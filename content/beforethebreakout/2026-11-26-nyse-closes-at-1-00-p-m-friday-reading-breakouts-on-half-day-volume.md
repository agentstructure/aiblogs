## No screens today, a short session tomorrow

US stock markets are closed today for Thanksgiving. The [New York Stock Exchange's holiday calendar](https://www.nyse.com/trade/hours-calendars) lists Thursday, November 26 as a market holiday and says each market will close early at 1:00 p.m. (1:15 p.m. for eligible options) on Friday, November 27. A regular NYSE core session runs from 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. ET, so Friday gives traders a little over half of a normal day.

That is a useful teaching moment for anyone who trades bases and breakouts. Nearly every rule of thumb about a clean breakout leans on volume, and volume is exactly what a holiday-shortened session distorts. A chart can print a new high on Friday that means very little, and a stock can slip back on a quiet tape for reasons that say nothing about its base.

This post is education, not a call on any stock. There is no fresh earnings story to chase today, which makes it a good day to review how to read the tape when the tape itself is unusual.

## Why volume is the confirming evidence

A base is a pause. Sellers who wanted out have mostly left, buyers are not yet willing to pay up, and the stock trades in a tightening range. The breakout is the moment demand overwhelms the supply that has been sitting at the top of that range.

Volume is how you tell the difference between a real surge in demand and a drift higher. Fidelity's technical analysis guide makes the general point when discussing support breaks: [a break on increasing volume indicates a stronger move than a break on low volume](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/trading-investing/technical-analysis/technical-indicator-guide/volume). The same logic applies to resistance. Fidelity's [cup-with-handle page](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/trading-investing/technical-analysis/technical-indicator-guide/cup-with-handle) says the breakout through the old resistance on the right side of the cup should occur with increased volume.

The educational site tastytrade is more specific. Its [cup-and-handle guide](https://tastytrade.com/learn/platforms-and-tools/charts/cup-and-handle/) says that on the breakout, volume should ideally expand to at least 50% above the average daily volume. It also warns that a breakout on thin volume has a higher rate of failure and may be a false breakout, and that a breakout on below-average volume should be treated as unconfirmed until volume expands. Other educators use different thresholds and averaging windows, so treat 50% as one published benchmark, not a law.

## The half-day problem

Here is where Friday matters. Volume is usually judged against an average daily figure. A session that ends at 1:00 p.m. has fewer hours in which to build that total, so a full-day volume count will tend to look light even if the stock saw real interest early.

That cuts two ways.

- **A breakout that looks weak may not be.** If a stock clears its pivot in the morning on heavy early trading, a low final volume figure may only reflect the shortened clock. Comparing the volume pace to the same hours on a normal day is more honest than comparing totals.
- **A breakout that looks strong may be an artifact.** Thin books move easily. A handful of orders can push a price through a level that would have held on a normal day, and the move can reverse once full participation returns the following Monday.

The simplest defense is patience. A breakout that matters will still be a breakout on Monday, November 30, when the full session returns. One that only worked because few people were trading will tend to fail that test.

## What to check on your charts this weekend

Use the quiet to audit the setups you follow rather than to hunt for new ones. A few questions help.

**Is the handle really dry?** In a cup with handle, tastytrade says volume contracts during the handle as sellers exhaust themselves. Look at the last several weeks. If volume has not shrunk as the range tightened, the base may still be absorbing supply and may not be ready.

**Is the handle the right shape?** Fidelity says the handle can retrace up to one-third of the cup's advance, but usually not more, and ideally forms over one to four weeks. The cup itself can take one to six months. A deep, sloppy handle or one that drags on for months is a weaker candidate than a shallow, tight one.

**Where is the pivot?** Write down the exact price a breakout would need to clear and the volume you would want to see, before the market opens, not after a candle is already moving. Deciding in advance keeps a thin-tape spike from talking you into a signal.

**What would failure look like?** tastytrade treats a close back inside the handle after an initial breakout as a failure signal, and notes that a failed setup does not automatically become a short opportunity. Knowing the failure condition ahead of time matters more on days when price action is erratic.

## Reading the failed breakout

Half-day sessions are a common place to see false starts, because the conditions that cause them, low participation and quick reversals, are built in. A failed breakout usually shows up as one of three patterns: a push above the pivot that closes back below it, volume that fails to expand on the push, or a quick reversal the next session on heavier volume than the breakout itself.

None of these is proof on its own. The point of reading volume is to build a weight of evidence. A breakout with expanding volume, a tight preceding base and a strong close is a different animal from one that pokes above a line on a quiet afternoon and fades.

## What to watch next

Friday's early close arrives at 1:00 p.m. ET, with options on eligible products trading until 1:15 p.m. Expect a compressed, lower-participation tape, and be careful about drawing conclusions from any single stock's move that day. The more informative test comes when the market reopens for a full session on Monday: do the stocks that moved on Friday hold their levels when the usual crowd returns, and does volume build as they do?

For swing traders, the practical takeaway is simple. A shortened session is a reason to lower the weight you give a single day's volume figure, not a reason to rewrite your rules. Keep the pivot, the volume benchmark and the failure condition you decided on earlier. If a setup is genuine, it will have the same shape, with the same tightening and dry-up, after the holiday as it did before it.
