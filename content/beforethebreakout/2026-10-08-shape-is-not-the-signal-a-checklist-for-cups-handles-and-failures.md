## Why most "textbook" bases are not textbook

Scroll any scanner output and you will find dozens of charts that look like a cup with a handle. Most of them are not. The pattern is easy to see in hindsight and hard to confirm while it is forming, a point [CMC Markets](https://www.cmcmarkets.com/en-gb/trading-strategy/what-is-cup-and-handle-pattern) lists among the pattern's known weaknesses: subjectivity and hindsight bias. Two traders can look at the same chart and disagree on whether a valid base exists.

The fix is to stop eyeballing shapes and start running a checklist. A base is a claim that sellers have been absorbed and supply is drying up. Each item below is a test of that claim. This is educational material about how patterns are read, not a recommendation on any stock.

## Test one: was there something to continue?

A cup with a handle is a continuation pattern. It needs a prior uptrend, because the whole idea is that a stock that was advancing paused, digested its gains and may resume. Both sources checked for this piece say the same thing. [Curved Trading](https://curvedtrading.com/articles/en/bullish/cup-and-handle-trading-strategy/) lists "no prior uptrend" as a failure mode, and CMC notes that a cup without a preceding advance may really be a rounding bottom or saucer, which is a different setup with different expectations.

Practical check: look left of the base. Did the stock make a meaningful advance, ideally to or near a prior high, before the base started? A base built under a long downtrend is a recovery attempt, not a continuation.

## Test two: is the cup rounded, and is it the right size?

CMC gives common guideposts: a cup depth of roughly 12% to 35% from the prior high, and a duration of about 7 weeks to 6 months. Very short formations carry less weight. The two rims should sit at roughly similar prices, with the left and right sides roughly symmetrical in time and price.

The bottom matters as much as the depth. A U-shape means the stock found buyers gradually. A sharp V suggests a base that was never fully built; Curved Trading says a V-shaped bottom "suggests a less stable base." Rounded does not guarantee success, but a V should lower your confidence before you even get to the handle.

These are rules of thumb, not laws. Depth and duration ranges are a guide to what is typical, and plenty of workable bases fall outside them. Treat the numbers as a reason to look harder when a chart is far outside them.

## Test three: does the handle behave?

The handle is the last shakeout before a move, and it is where many lookalikes fail. Per CMC, it should form in the upper part of the cup's range, drift sideways or slightly lower, and last roughly 1 to 4 weeks, shorter than the cup itself.

Handle depth is where the sources differ, which is worth knowing. CMC's body text cites no more than 10% to 15% from the right-side high, while its own summary table says less than half the cup's depth. Curved Trading uses the half-of-cup standard. Both agree on the direction of the warning: a handle that retraces more than half the cup, forms in the lower half of the range, or runs longer than the cup itself is a red flag. A deep, long handle looks less like a pause and more like a second leg down.

## Test four: what did volume do?

Volume is the part of the picture a price-only screen cannot show you. The expected signature, described by both sources, runs in three stages:

1. Volume fades as the stock sells off into the bottom of the cup.
2. Volume rises as the stock recovers along the right side.
3. Volume contracts and stays light through the handle.

That last stage is the volume dry-up. It says the remaining holders are not selling at these prices, and the stock is quiet because few people want to leave. What you want to see is tight daily ranges and below-average volume in the handle, not a stock that is quiet because nobody cares, and not one that is quiet with a few heavy down days mixed in. A dry-up that is interrupted by high-volume selling is not a dry-up.

A quick way to judge it: compare the handle's average volume to the cup's average. If the handle's volume is not visibly lower, the supply you are hoping has dried up may still be there.

## Test five: what happens on the breakout day?

The standard trigger is a move above the handle's resistance line. Confirmation, per CMC, is a close above that level rather than an intraday poke, together with a volume increase on the breakout session. Curved Trading treats volume expansion as the single most important confirmation and says a light-volume breakout is more prone to failure.

This is where bases earn their reputation. A breakout on weak volume tells you buyers were not in a hurry. It may work anyway, but the evidence is thinner, and the chart gives you less reason to trust it.

## How failed breakouts show themselves

Both sources describe the classic failure: price clears the handle, draws in breakout buyers, then reverses back below the level and traps them. The warning signs tend to appear quickly:

- **Light-volume breakout.** No surge of participation on the day price cleared resistance.
- **Failed retest.** Curved Trading notes that a retest of the handle high that holds on light volume can offer a lower-risk second entry, while a retest that falls back into the handle signals a likely false breakout.
- **Close back inside the base.** A close below the breakout level shortly after the move is the cleanest sign that the setup is not working.
- **Hostile market backdrop.** CMC cautions that the pattern may fail in ranging or volatile markets. A good-looking base inside a choppy tape has a worse record than the same base in a trending one.

Where traders place the invalidation point varies. The sources list stops below the handle low (tighter, more exposed to noise), below the cup's midpoint, or below the cup low (wider). The point is not which one is right. It is to define the level where the thesis is wrong before the trade, so the decision is not made in real time under stress. Curved Trading's worked example uses a handle low as the invalidation level and measures reward against that risk, producing roughly a 2.2:1 reward-to-risk setup; the numbers are illustrative, not a forecast.

## A short checklist to run before the chart gets exciting

- Prior uptrend present?
- Rounded cup, rims at similar levels, depth and duration in a reasonable range?
- Handle in the upper half, short, shallow relative to the cup?
- Volume lower in the handle than in the cup?
- If it breaks out: close above the handle high, on visibly higher volume?
- Is there a defined level where the idea is wrong?

## What to watch next

Use the list on your own watchlist this week. Pull up the names you flagged as "bases" and score each one honestly against the five tests. Expect many to fail at least one. That is the point: the stocks that pass every test are rare, and the ones that fail early, on a V-shaped bottom or a sloppy handle, are cheap lessons rather than costly ones. Then keep a log of breakouts that failed, noting which test they had passed narrowly. Your own record of near-misses will teach more than any pattern gallery.
