## What Kongsberg reported

Norway's Kongsberg Gruppen (KOG) reported third-quarter results on Thursday, Oct. 29, and the headline numbers were strong. According to the company's [regulatory release](https://www.inderes.fi/en/releases/q3-2026-kongsberg-delivers-a-strong-third-quarter-with-a-record-high-order-backlog), revenue rose 50% to NOK 11.3 billion and operating profit (EBIT) rose 66% to NOK 1.9 billion. Order intake came in at NOK 23.3 billion, against NOK 9.5 billion in the same quarter last year, a book-to-bill ratio of 2.06. That means Kongsberg booked about two kroner of new work for every krone it delivered.

The order backlog reached a record NOK 174.2 billion, up NOK 16.7 billion in the quarter. Nine-month order intake is close to NOK 67 billion, according to the release and the [earnings call summary](https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-kongsberg-q3-2026-revenue-jumps-50-on-record-orders-93CH-4981707).

Kongsberg's 2025 revenue was NOK 32.8 billion, per the same release. By simple division, the current backlog is more than five times last year's sales. That is a calculation from the company's figures, not a number Kongsberg reports itself. It also mixes a full year of revenue with a quarter-end backlog, so treat it as a rough scale check.

## The contracts behind the number

One award did a lot of the work. Belgium signed a NASAMS air defence agreement worth about NOK 10 billion, which the release says makes Belgium the 18th NASAMS nation. The call summary describes it as the largest NASAMS contract to date. NASAMS is the medium-range air defence system Kongsberg builds with Raytheon (RTX), and it is one of the systems European governments are buying as they rebuild air defence stocks.

Other awards in the quarter included:

- NOK 1.5 billion from the Netherlands for NASAMS.
- NOK 1.1 billion from the Canadian Army for Protector remote weapon stations.
- A US Air Force order for additional Joint Strike Missiles for the F-35 fleet, which the call summary values at $100 million.
- $65 million from the US government for Rusty Dagger missiles from Zone 5, a US missile maker Kongsberg acquired earlier this year.
- $54 million from the US government for autonomous underwater vehicles.

The mix matters for investors. This is not only European rearmament. Part of the quarter's order growth came from US customers and from Canada, which suggests demand for missiles and air defence is broader than the NATO budget story alone.

## Why the delivery schedule is the real story

The most useful detail for anyone assessing the rearmament cycle is not the backlog total but when it turns into revenue. Per the call summary, about 7% of the backlog, roughly NOK 12 billion, is scheduled for delivery in the fourth quarter of 2026. About 30% falls in 2027, and 63%, about NOK 109 billion, falls in 2028 or later. The summary gives the 2027 amount as both NOK 52.3 billion and NOK 53 billion in different places, so the exact figure is uncertain.

Backlog is a promise, not income. A backlog that mostly ships after 2028 gives Kongsberg multi-year visibility. It also means the company must hire, tool up and source components for years before most of that work is paid for. Management appears to know this. CEO Eirik Lie said, "Our focus now is on executing on our strong order backlog."

The capacity plans show the scale of the effort, based on the call summary:

- A new factory in Gdansk, Poland, with production expected to start in the first half of 2028.
- Missile factories in Fort Worth, Texas and in Australia, which management says are on track.
- A 45,000 square meter undersea production site in Horten, Norway, which has recently been occupied.

The Gdansk date is a reminder that orders placed now are often filled from capacity that does not yet exist.

## Margins and cash: the cost of growth

Profitability held up, but the segments tell a more mixed story. The call summary reports Defence Systems revenue of NOK 5.4 billion, up 58%, with about a 20.6% margin. Missiles & Aerostructures revenue grew 87% to NOK 4.0 billion, but its margin fell to 11.6% from 16.5% a year earlier. Management attributed the dilution to the Zone 5 ramp-up and deal costs, and expects Zone 5 margins to match the rest of the group by 2029.

Cash flow was healthy. The summary reports operating cash flow of NOK 4.1 billion and quarter-end cash of NOK 6.4 billion, up from NOK 4.9 billion. Acquisitions, including Sonatech, and capacity investment were the main uses of cash.

Note that Kongsberg gave no numerical guidance for 2026 or 2027. Management said it expects "strong revenue growth" in 2027, according to the summary. The call summary has some internal inconsistencies, so treat its segment figures as approximate until the full report is checked.

## How it compares with Thales

Kongsberg is not the only European name showing this pattern. On Oct. 22, Thales (HO) reported nine-month order intake of EUR 19.1 billion, up 15% organically, according to the [earnings call summary](https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-thales-q3-2026-order-growth-lifts-outlook-93CH-4964832). Defence order intake was EUR 11.3 billion, up 14%, and defence sales were EUR 9.4 billion, up 13%. Thales guided to full-year organic sales growth at the upper end of 6% to 7%, potentially beyond, and a book-to-bill ratio above 1.1.

The two companies are at different stages. Thales is a much larger, diversified group whose book-to-bill is above 1 but nowhere near 2. Kongsberg's ratio is far higher because its order intake is lumpy and driven by a few large government contracts. A quarter like this one can look very different next time if a large NASAMS-sized award does not repeat.

The pattern across both reports is that orders are running ahead of deliveries. The constraint on European defence revenue is increasingly production capacity, not government demand.

## What to watch

Three things will show whether the backlog converts.

First, Kongsberg's quarterly revenue growth against its backlog. The company expects strong growth in 2027, and each quarter should show whether the factory build-out is keeping pace. Management also says testing of the FREYJA anti-ballistic missile is targeted to start by year-end.

Second, other European primes. Rheinmetall (RHM) had a busy week on the order side. A [market news report](https://www.ad-hoc-news.de/boerse/news/corporate-news/rheinmetall-stock-schakal-order-lifts-programme-to-291-vehicles/70434020) says OCCAR placed an order for 69 additional Schakal infantry fighting vehicles for Germany and the Netherlands, bringing the programme to 291 vehicles, with Rheinmetall's share of the option call-offs in the "medium three-digit million-euro range." That report puts Rheinmetall's Xetra close at EUR 1,048.20 on Oct. 29. Other outlets date the OCCAR option exercise to August, so the timing of that order is unclear and we treat it as context rather than a fresh catalyst. The same report points to Rheinmetall's third-quarter results on Nov. 5, which I could not confirm on the company's own site.

Third, whether the new capacity in Poland, Texas and Australia opens on schedule. Management lists delivery risk and the timing of factory openings among the main risks.
