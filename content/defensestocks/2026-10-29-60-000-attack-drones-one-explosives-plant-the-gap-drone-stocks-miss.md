## What happened

The Pentagon's plan to buy tens of thousands of cheap attack drones has a supply problem that has little to do with drones. [A Wall Street Journal report, relayed this week](https://www.businessreport.com/the-pentagon-has-a-drone-problem-not-enough-explosives-to-arm-them/), says the military does not have enough explosive fill to arm them. The main domestic source of HMX and RDX is the Holston Army Ammunition Plant in Tennessee, an 84-year-old facility that makes about 3% of its 1944 peak output, according to the report. It carries a one- to two-year backlog.

The Army committed $1 billion to upgrade Holston, but that work has slipped to 2029 or later. A new research facility in Kentucky is expected to lift HMX and RDX output before 2031. [DroneXL's summary](https://dronexl.co/2026/10/28/pentagon-attack-drones-explosives-shortage-odin-energetics/) puts that facility at $903 million and notes that the Pentagon has also created an energetics production office.

The demand side is large. The Drone Dominance Program is a $1.1 billion effort to field more than 200,000 small drones by 2027, [per Army Recognition](https://www.armyrecognition.com/news/army-news/2026/u-s-orders-6-000-striker-indoor-attack-drones-for-40-5-million-under-drone-dominance-program). The Journal reports that the 60,000 attack drones now committed are designed to carry explosives. Hegseth's July 2025 directive classified small drones as consumables, which means they are bought to be used up.

## A startup claims a workaround

The one new supplier in the story is Odin Energetics, a joint venture of Solugen and Kraken Kinetics that has committed $115 million. Its executives say one formulation is 30% more powerful than the explosives now used on military drones. DroneXL adds that Odin claims it can already make close to a tenth of Holston's roughly 11 million pounds of HMX and RDX a year, and could supply the military in small quantities next year.

Treat all of that as company claims. DroneXL reports the Army is in talks with Odin and that the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital has discussed funding, but no contract has been announced. Neither the Journal nor the Gauntlet II results name Kraken Kinetics as a supplier to any competitor.

## The winners are all private

The explosives gap lands on a program whose winners are not stocks. [Inside Unmanned Systems](https://insideunmannedsystems.com/nine-companies-win-ddp-gauntlet-2-fpv-drone-competition/) reports that the Gauntlet II top five in each mission area were named on Sept. 17: Perennial Autonomy, Hyperscale, Neros, Skycutter and Swarm Defense Technologies in Deep Strike, and Neros, Orqa U.S., XTEND, Vector Defense and ModalAI in Close Quarters Battle. Neros is the only company in both lists. [The Defense News](https://www.thedefensenews.com/Pentagon-Orders-60000-Kamikaze-Drones-Under-Second-Phase-of-Drone-Dominance-Program/) lists projected allocations ranging from 4,000 to 8,000 drones per company per category, with Neros at 14,000 combined.

None of those names trades publicly. The listed drone group is trading on sentiment instead. On Oct. 27, [24/7 Wall St. reported](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/10/27/drone-stocks-rally-as-high-beta-small-caps-lead-red-cat-jumps-10-ondas-climbs-8-aerovironment-ticks-up/) Red Cat (RCAT) at $4.84 after a 10% afternoon gain, Ondas (ONDS) at $6.66 up 8%, and AeroVironment (AVAV) at $128.03 up 2%. Year to date, those three were down 39%, 32% and 47% respectively.

## Orders are not obligations

The 60,000 figure is a ceiling. Inside Unmanned Systems cautions that the maximum is not guaranteed, noting that Gauntlet I ordered 22,320 drones of a planned 30,000. It also says the program has not confirmed final quantities for Orqa U.S., XTEND, Hyperscale or ModalAI.

Some orders are real. [Army Recognition reports](https://www.armyrecognition.com/news/army-news/2026/u-s-orders-6-000-striker-indoor-attack-drones-for-40-5-million-under-drone-dominance-program) a $40.5 million Department of Defense order to XTEND for 6,000 Striker drones, or about $6,750 each, though the packages may not include identical equipment. Even so, the Army's own framing is cautious. Jon Strzelec, the Army's small-drone manufacturing lead, said the Pentagon is trying to ["artificially induce" demand](https://dronexl.co/2026/10/26/army-artificial-drone-demand-signal-pdw-year-of-orders/) so U.S. companies will build a domestic supply chain. Performance Drone Works CEO James Slider told the same coverage that makers want sustained, normalized demand, and he has asked for one award followed by 1,000 drones a month for a year.

## What comes next

Phase III is the next test. [DroneLife reports](https://dronelife.com/2026/10/14/drone-dominance-phase-iii-gauntlet-2-5/) a $450 million round for about 60,000 one-way attack drones with an option for up to 40,000 more, at fixed prices of $5,000 per Deep Strike drone and $3,000 per Close Quarters Battle drone, excluding munitions and support equipment. The solicitation closed on Oct. 12 and Gauntlet III testing is expected in March 2027. Applicants must show they can build at least 8,000 drones within five months.

Those prices exclude munitions, so the explosives question sits outside the headline unit cost. Separately, Gauntlet 2.5, a reusable bomber drone trial, was pushed from October to early November, with 17 companies invited and about $32 million expected for initial prototype orders.

Money is the other open item. On Oct. 14, [Federal News Network reported](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/defense-main/2026/10/hegseth-ties-new-drone-command-to-uncertain-reconciliation-bill/) that Hegseth is working with Congress to secure more than $50 billion for autonomy and robotics through a reconciliation bill with no clear path to approval. The comptroller said officials could shift the request into the base budget, which would likely require cuts elsewhere. Midterm elections are Nov. 3.

## Where listed companies fit

The public exposure is indirect. BAE Systems (BA.) has operated Holston [since 1999, according to the company](https://www.baesystems.com/en-us/article/finalizing-major-modernization-at-holston-army-ammunition-plant), so Army decisions on the plant's upgrade schedule and any alternative energetics suppliers matter to it. This post does not claim a financial impact, and none of the sources quantify one.

L3Harris (LHX), which reports earnings today, sits on the counter-drone side. [Army Technology reports](https://www.army-technology.com/news/l3harris-drone-interceptor-contract/) it won a contract worth up to $60 million over three years for the Army's Xtended Range Counter-sUAS interceptor, a ramjet-powered missile now entering flight testing of a fully integrated design. That is development money, not a production order.

What to watch: whether the Army or the Office of Strategic Capital signs with Odin or another energetics supplier; whether Phase III awards turn into obligated contracts rather than ceilings; how the reconciliation fight resolves after Nov. 3; and whether any public drone maker qualifies for Phase III. For now, the units are being ordered faster than the explosives to fill them are being made.
