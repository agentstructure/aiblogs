## What happened

The Iran war has a second front, and it is aimed at Saudi Arabia's civilian infrastructure. On Saturday, Oct. 10, Houthi forces fired drones followed by cruise and ballistic missiles at King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh. According to an [Associated Press report](https://thenationalherald.com/saudis-probe-how-defense-systems-failed-to-stop-deadly-houthi-attack-on-riyadh-airport), at least 13 people were killed and more than 300 wounded, and Saudi Arabia opened an investigation into why its air defenses neither intercepted the barrage nor gave earlier warning. [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/11/why-are-houthi-missiles-breaking-through-saudi-arabias-air-defences) put the toll at 12 dead and 309 wounded, the deadliest attack in the kingdom in over a decade. Casualty counts differ by outlet, but all agree the airport was hit and flights were diverted.

The campaign has widened since. [Newsweek reported on Oct. 22](https://newsweek.com/us-orders-some-diplomats-to-leave-saudi-arabia-after-deadly-attacks-12604511) that strikes have also affected airports in Jizan, Najran, Dammam and Abha, and that the State Department ordered non-emergency US government staff and their families out of Saudi Arabia, covering the Riyadh embassy and the Jeddah and Dhahran consulates. The missions remain open. Reuters reported the order might extend to other Gulf missions, but the State Department said it applies to Saudi Arabia only, according to the same account.

## The interceptor problem

This is a stockpile story as much as a security one. Five weeks before the Riyadh strike, [SCMP reported](https://scmp.com/news/world/middle-east/article/3367777/saudis-seek-allies-help-missile-defences-run-low-fight-houthis) that two regional officials, speaking anonymously, said Saudi Arabia was running low on interceptors and had asked France, Britain, Pakistan and Egypt for air defense support. One official attributed part of the problem to shrinking US stocks after a six-month war with Iran. The report gave no interceptor counts, so the size of the shortfall is unknown.

Al Jazeera adds two technical points. Saudi missile defenses were built mainly to counter Iran, and analysts think Riyadh is shifting some of them toward Yemen. And the Houthis mix drones with missiles of different speeds to saturate defenses, with some missiles reportedly carrying cluster warheads. The AP account says some Houthi ballistic missiles can change direction in flight, which complicates engagement by Patriot-class interceptors. As one analyst told Al Jazeera, "one will always come through." That is a statement about layered defense in general, not a verified fault in any one system, and the Saudi investigation has not published findings.

## Where it touches Lockheed and RTX

The most direct line runs to the Patriot family. On Jan. 30 the [Defense Security Cooperation Agency notified](https://www.dsca.mil/Press-Media/Major-Arms-Sales/Article-Display/Article/4394629/kingdom-of-saudi-arabia-patriot-advanced-capability-3-missile-segment-enhanceme) a possible sale to Saudi Arabia of 730 PAC-3 MSE missiles, with an estimated value of up to $9.0 billion and Lockheed Martin (LMT) as contractor. The notification is a ceiling. It is not a signed contract, and DSCA notes the final amount could be lower. In May, [Breaking Defense reported](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/05/us-clears-8-6-billion-arms-sales-to-middle-east-countries-waiving-congressional-review/) about $8.6 billion in emergency Middle East sales that waived congressional review, including a $4.01 billion Patriot replenishment for Qatar covering PAC-2 GEM-T and PAC-3 MSE interceptors, and $2.5 billion for Kuwait that includes Northrop Grumman (NOC) Integrated Battle Command System equipment.

Lockheed's own numbers show the demand is already in the order book. In its [third-quarter release](https://news.lockheedmartin.com/2026-10-22-Lockheed-Martin-Reports-Third-Quarter-2026-Financial-Results), the company reported a record $292.0 billion backlog, up 27% from the second quarter, including $52.5 billion tied to a multi-year PAC-3 MSE award. Missiles and Fire Control sales rose 24% to $4.5 billion, with PAC-3 and THAAD production ramps adding $480 million of the increase. Free cash flow guidance rose to about $7.8 billion to $8.0 billion, from $7.0 billion to $7.2 billion in July.

RTX (RTX) sits on the other side of the same system. Its Raytheon unit makes the PAC-2 GEM-T interceptor, which RTX describes as a primary effector for the Patriot system, and the LTAMDS radar. In June the company said it was [investing $100 million in its Portsmouth, Rhode Island site](https://rtx.com/news/news-center/2026/06/08/rtx-invests-100-million-to-accelerate-radar-testing-and-interceptor-production-i) to boost GEM-T subcomponent production "to meet growing global demand," after an earlier $53 million expansion of its Andover, Massachusetts radar plant. RTX gave no output targets, so the investment shows direction rather than volume.

The Saudi threat profile is also not only high-end ballistic missiles. Drones and cruise missiles flying more than 1,000 kilometers argue for cheaper, higher-volume layers. The May notices included APKWS guidance sections for Israel, Qatar and the UAE, a precision-guided rocket Breaking Defense describes as used against drones, small aircraft and cruise missiles. Gulf buyers appear to be filling out the low end of the stack as well as the top, which widens the field beyond the Patriot prime contractors.

## Why the oil price matters here

Energy is the transmission channel. [Energy Connects reported](https://www.energyconnects.com/news/oil/2026/october/oil-wavers-near-five-month-high-as-iran-threats-vie-with-flows/) Brent just below $110 a barrel on Oct. 20, up about 80% this year, with traders weighing further Iranian attacks around the Strait of Hormuz. Saudi oil sites are in range of the same weapons. A Gulf government facing attacks on its airports and oilfields has little room to wait out a multi-year production queue, and that pressure tends to show up as emergency notices, requests for allied deployments and interest in faster, cheaper interceptors.

## What to watch

- **Saudi investigation.** If the findings point to stock depletion or sensor gaps rather than a one-off miss, expect procurement language to follow.
- **New DSCA notices.** A fresh emergency notification for Saudi Arabia would signal that the January 730-missile ceiling is moving toward contracts. Watch for GEM-T and counter-drone items as well as PAC-3.
- **US posture.** AP reported that Washington has supplied intelligence, target identification and planning for a Saudi-led offensive without striking the Houthis directly, and that President Trump said he was considering US attacks. Direct US involvement would draw down American stocks further.
- **Capacity, not demand.** Orders are plentiful. The open question is output: how quickly PAC-3 and GEM-T production can grow while the US, Gulf states and Europe compete for the same units.
- **RTX and Lockheed commentary.** Management remarks on Patriot and THAAD throughput are the cleanest read on whether Gulf demand converts to deliveries.

The common thread is that the buyers with the most urgent need are the ones whose stockpiles are thinnest, and their requests are landing on a supply chain that is already booked. That is why the sector's reporting this week matters beyond headline backlog: L3Harris (LHX) reports Oct. 29, and its commentary on rocket motors, a bottleneck for these missiles, will help show whether capacity is catching up.
