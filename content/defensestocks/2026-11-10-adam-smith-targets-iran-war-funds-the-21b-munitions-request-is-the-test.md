## What Democrats said

Incoming House Armed Services Committee chairman Adam Smith (D-Wash.) said this week that Democrats will move to end funding for the Iran war once they control Congress. Asked on The New Republic's podcast whether Democrats in both chambers would pass a measure forbidding that funding, he answered "Absolutely" and called it ["one of the first things we do,"](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/rep-adam-smith-says-dems-164005079.html) according to a HuffPost report published Nov. 6. Bloomberg reported separately that Democrats [plan to cut war funding early](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/articles/trump-1-5-trillion-defense-144026439.html) and could back roughly $1 trillion for defense overall, versus the $1.5 trillion fiscal 2027 request.

Two caveats matter. First, this is a stated intention, not a bill. The HuffPost report names no vehicle or vote schedule, and Democrats take their majorities only when the new Congress convenes. Second, the lame-duck session that began Monday is still run by the current majority.

For investors, the useful question is narrower than "war or no war." Which parts of the Iran-related spending are really war operations, and which are restocking? Those two buckets can be treated very differently by the same Congress.

## The $21B line in the request

The figure in this headline comes from the White House's June 24 supplemental request. [PBS NewsHour reported](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/white-house-seeks-87-6b-from-congress-for-iran-war-costs-u-s-farmers-and-ebola-response) that the request totals $87.6B. About $67B of that is for the Defense Department, and $21B of the defense portion is for weapons munitions. The same report lists $17.3B for operational costs and $12.1B for classified programs.

PBS did not name which munitions the $21B would buy. The package also carries non-defense items, including $11.1B in aid for farmers and $1.4B for an Ebola response, which gives opponents more to object to. Congress has not approved the request, and PBS reported it was unclear whether lawmakers would take it up at all.

Senator Patty Murray (D-Wash.) said she would not "rubberstamp tens of billions more for this disastrous war of choice." Reps. Tom Cole (R-Okla.) and Ken Calvert (R-Calif.) said in a joint statement that "our defense strength must be maintained, not merely demonstrated." That split is the fault line. The same dollars can be described as paying for a war or as rebuilding depleted inventory.

## Why replenishment may behave differently

The Congressional Budget Office estimated in September that the war had cost the Defense Department [more than $38B through Aug. 1](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/iran-war-has-cost-the-u-s-more-than-38-billion-so-far-budget-office-says), and could keep costing up to $3B a month or more depending on intensity. PBS reported that the estimate comes largely from replacing munitions and equipment lost in battle. CBO also said its figures are "subject to considerable uncertainty," and that the Pentagon declined to answer its information request.

Forecast International's Nov. 6 analysis argues that [munitions replenishment, counter-drone systems, Navy shipbuilding and autonomous systems](https://dsm.forecastinternational.com/2026/11/06/midterm-results-trigger-a-showdown-over-defense-spending/) are the areas most likely to keep bipartisan support, though at debatable funding levels. That is one analyst's view, not a vote count, but it matches the logic above: a stockpile that has already been fired is hard to leave empty.

The same analysis notes a separate exposure. A reconciliation package the Pentagon had been counting on would have raised fiscal 2027 munitions funding to $76.3B, up 184.7% from fiscal 2026, with Patriot, THAAD, SM-3, SM-6 and hypersonic weapons named as targets. With Democrats opposed to the roughly $350B reconciliation piece of the request, Bloomberg described that route as [looking doomed](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/articles/trump-1-5-trillion-defense-144026439.html). If so, replenishment would have to move into the regular budget or a supplemental.

## What it means for Lockheed and RTX

Lockheed Martin (LMT) and RTX (RTX) are the most direct readers of this debate because they build the interceptors the conflict has consumed. Tech Times, citing the Payne Institute, reported that [about 198 THAAD interceptors were fired in the first 16 days](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/319033/20260625/iran-war-supplemental-pentagon-requests-876b-munitions-stockpiles-run-low.htm) of the conflict, roughly 40% of the inventory at the start. The same article cites a seven-year, $35.3B sole-source Missile Defense Agency contract to expand Lockheed's THAAD production, and a CSIS assessment that fully restoring the stockpile could take three to eight years.

Contract ceilings are not cash, though. Whether those multi-year production plans turn into steady obligations depends on annual appropriations, and the stopgap that currently funds the government runs out Dec. 11. A long continuing resolution can delay production increases unless Congress grants exceptions, which Forecast International lists as a risk to several new programs.

Defense shares also reacted to the election itself. Reporting on the size of the Nov. 4 declines varies between intraday and closing figures, so this post does not rely on a number. Bloomberg's account says only that Lockheed and Northrop Grumman (NOC) [fell during the week](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/articles/trump-1-5-trillion-defense-144026439.html) as expectations for a spending surge weakened.

## The battlefield backdrop

A funding cutoff would arrive while the conflict is unresolved. CBS News live coverage from Nov. 2-3 described [limited U.S. defensive strikes on Qeshm Island](https://www.cbsnews.com/live-updates/iran-war-us-trump-negotiations-midterms/) after the IRGC fired on a U.S. aircraft, Iranian officials saying the Strait of Hormuz would reopen only if seven conditions are met, and Qatar working toward an interim deal. The same coverage cited a record 30 attacks on commercial vessels in October, per UKMTO.

That uncertainty cuts both ways. A deal would weaken the case for war spending, but it would not refill magazines. Continued fighting would draw down more stock while making new war money harder to pass.

## What to watch

- **Whether the $87.6B request moves.** Any committee action on the $21B munitions line, and whether it is separated from the farm, Ebola and other non-defense items.
- **Where replenishment lands.** Watch for munitions language in a stopgap, anomaly requests, or a revised base budget before Dec. 11.
- **The vehicle for a funding ban.** Smith has described intent, not text. Standalone legislation and a rider on must-pass legislation face different odds.
- **Munitions orders.** New awards for THAAD, Patriot and other interceptors would show whether the Pentagon is committing money regardless of the supplemental's fate.
