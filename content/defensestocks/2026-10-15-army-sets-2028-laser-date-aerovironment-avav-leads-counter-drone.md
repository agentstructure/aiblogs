## What happened

The U.S. Army has put a date on its first fielded laser weapons for shooting down drones. According to [Defense News](https://www.defensenews.com/industry/techwatch/2026/10/13/us-army-targets-2028-for-first-high-energy-laser-counter-drone-weapons/), the service is aiming to field the first systems under its Enduring-High Energy Laser (E-HEL) program by spring 2028 at the latest, with a hoped-for window of December to January 2028.

The company behind the hardware is AeroVironment (AVAV). In September the Army [awarded it $464.8 million](https://www.defensenews.com/industry/techwatch/2026/09/03/us-army-launches-laser-production-with-465m-contract-award/) for production of its LOCUST laser under an Other Transaction Agreement. Defense News describes the order as dozens of lasers, and its October report puts the counter-drone contract at more than 26 weapons units.

This is a production award, not another prototype round. That distinction is what makes it worth following for investors in the emerging defense-tech names.

## The schedule is the story

Defense News reports that the first LOCUST X3, a modular, palletized laser, is due for delivery in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2027. It will mount first on the Joint Light Tactical Vehicle (JLTV) and can also be integrated onto the Infantry Squad Vehicle.

The Army also said it recorded eight kills with the prototype during Army Modernization Day this month. Eight kills is a small sample, and the Army has not published the conditions, so it shows the system works rather than how well it works in combat.

The Army's own officials are pushing for speed. Brig. Gen. Guy Yelverton, who runs the Defensive Fires portfolio, said the aim is to "give it to soldiers as soon as possible." Kirk Williams of the Army's Directed Energy Project Office added that "everything that we produce as a capable counter-UAS system tends to get deployed somewhere." The 7th Infantry Division was named as a possible partner unit, though Defense News says the deployment location is unclear.

## Why lasers, and why now

The economic argument is the one defense analysts keep returning to: drones are cheap, and the interceptors used against them often are not. A laser trades a high up-front cost for a very low marginal cost per shot. Defense News did not state a cost per shot for LOCUST, so that figure remains unconfirmed here. The same report gives the system's power as 30 kilowatts.

AeroVironment's John Garrity framed the demand simply: "drones aren't getting any slower and our adversaries aren't getting any lazier with them."

Lasers also come with a track record to be honest about. Defense News notes that the Army's earlier DE M-SHORAD laser prototypes, built for the Stryker vehicle, drew disappointing soldier feedback from the Middle East. E-HEL builds instead on AeroVironment's AMP-HEL prototypes, which soldiers already use. A different platform, a different power architecture and a production contract do not guarantee better reliability in dust, heat and weather. Col. Charles Kean of the 7th Infantry Division cautioned that directed energy is not a "single bullet" solution, which fits how the Army describes it: one layer next to guns, missiles and electronic warfare.

## The drone side of the budget is being reorganized too

Lasers are the defensive half of a Pentagon push on small drones. [DefenseScoop reported](https://defensescoop.com/2026/10/14/military-drone-tech-task-force-easy) that a new Task Force Easy, commanded by Army Col. Michael Penn, is working to iterate on weaponized small drones and small uncrewed ground vehicles. Penn said the department is "slower, less effective" at adapting technology than its adversaries, and that "the solution isn't just drone dominance — it's that adaptation dominance." His line "It's not just about buying 1,000 drones" suggests the buyer is looking at how fast software, tactics and hardware can be updated, not unit counts alone.

The same article says Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced policies in September that include a plan for a four-star combatant command to lead autonomous warfare. For listed companies, the practical read is that organizational ownership of drones and counter-drone work is consolidating. That can speed purchasing, and it can also concentrate it among fewer vendors.

## What the financials show

AeroVironment is not a pure laser story. In its [fiscal 2027 first-quarter report](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20260910537472/en/AeroVironment-Announces-Fiscal-2027-First-Quarter-Results) for the quarter ended August 1, 2026, the company reported:

- Revenue of $480.5 million, up 6% from $454.7 million a year earlier.
- Bookings of $0.7 billion, a book-to-bill ratio of 1.4.
- Funded backlog of $1.5 billion, up 37% year over year.
- A net loss of $5.1 million, or $0.10 per share, compared with a $67.4 million loss a year ago.
- Non-GAAP EPS of $0.59, versus $0.32.

It maintained fiscal 2027 guidance of $2.125 billion to $2.225 billion in revenue and $3.02 to $3.34 in non-GAAP EPS. Against that revenue base, a $464.8 million award is meaningful, but it is a multi-period production contract and the first delivery is not due until late fiscal 2027, so it does not land as revenue all at once. The company's own figures also show GAAP profit is still thin, which is worth keeping in mind when reading non-GAAP numbers.

## What to watch

- **Delivery of the first LOCUST X3.** Slippage against the fourth quarter of fiscal 2027 target would be the clearest early warning.
- **Published test data.** Kill counts under defined conditions, such as target type, range and weather, would turn the eight-kill demonstration into evidence.
- **Follow-on quantities.** The Army describes E-HEL as an enduring program, so later orders and any foreign sales matter more than the first lot.
- **Competing approaches.** Lasers are one layer. Interceptor missiles, guns and electronic warfare compete for the same counter-drone dollars, and the Army's budget choices will show how much weight lasers get.
- **Task Force Easy outputs.** Standards and procedures set there could decide which small-drone vendors become program-of-record suppliers.
