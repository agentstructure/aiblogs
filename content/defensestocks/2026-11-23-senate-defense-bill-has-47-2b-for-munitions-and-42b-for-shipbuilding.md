## What happened

Senate Republican appropriators released their fiscal 2027 defense bill last week, and the line items show a clear priority: missiles first, ships second. The [committee's bill summary](https://www.appropriations.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/def_fy27_bill_summary.pdf) describes "nearly $1.1 trillion" in discretionary funding for the Defense Department and the intelligence community. Inside that total sit $47.2 billion for additional munitions procurement and acceleration, and $42 billion for shipbuilding.

The bill is a committee proposal, not law. [ExecutiveGov](https://www.executivegov.com/articles/senate-1-trillion-fy-2027-defense-appropriations-bill) notes that most federal programs are running under a continuing resolution at fiscal 2026 levels through Dec. 11, and that the stopgap bars the department from starting new production. Sen. Mitch McConnell, who chairs the Senate defense appropriations subcommittee, called the bill a "roadmap" for "generational investments in the national defense," according to [Anadolu Agency](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/americas/us-senate-republicans-unveil-nearly-11t-defense-spending-bill-for-fy2027/4127218).

One structural choice matters for investors. The committee says nearly $69 billion that the administration first requested as one-time mandatory spending is instead provided as discretionary funds, "to provide stable, sustainable, and predictable funding." That moves money out of the reconciliation process, whose outcome is uncertain, and into the annual bill.

## The nine-munition multiyear list

The munitions money is concentrated. The summary provides $40.6 billion for new multiyear procurement contracts covering nine munitions: AMRAAM, GMLRS, PAC-3 MSE, the Precision Strike Missile Increment 1, Standard Missile-3 Block IB, Standard Missile-3 Block IIA, Standard Missile-6, THAAD interceptors and Tomahawk. A further $1.5 billion adds quantities under existing multiyear contracts for LRASM and JASSM-ER.

Multiyear contracts matter because they let suppliers commit to long-lead parts and capacity with a stated buyer behind them. The committee also moves multiyear contracts that "should be funded on an annual basis" into the base bill rather than leaving them in a mandatory request.

Lockheed Martin (LMT) is the prime on PAC-3 MSE, THAAD, GMLRS, PrSM, LRASM and JASSM-ER, and RTX (RTX) is the prime on AMRAAM, the Standard Missile family and Tomahawk. Those prime assignments are general industry knowledge rather than something the committee summary states. The summary itself names only the missiles, so any read-through to a specific company's revenue depends on contract awards that have not been made yet.

The bill also pushes beyond the incumbents' product lines. It provides $1.9 billion for "new entrant" munitions such as Blackbeard Ground Launch, MACE, the Family of Affordable Mass Missile, low-cost cruise missiles and low-cost containerized munitions, plus $1 billion for additional Joint Advanced Tactical Missile quantities and $1.4 billion to replenish stocks of items such as JDAM, APKWS and 30mm proximity ammunition. Smaller and newer producers have a stated funding line to compete for.

## Interceptors are the largest single block

Air and missile defense gets $35.6 billion, and $32 billion of that is for "additional defensive interceptors." The bill adds $812 million for two more THAAD radars and $170 million for air and missile defense software. It also puts $9.4 billion in discretionary funds toward Golden Dome efforts the administration had requested as mandatory spending.

For comparison, the committee provides $11.1 billion for F-35 procurement, modifications and sustainment, including 15 more F-35Cs, five more F-35Bs and five more F-35As. Drone and counter-drone work gets $6.2 billion, including $3 billion for the Defense Autonomous Working Group and $2 billion for the interagency task force on drone defense.

The bill also supports Space Force and Air Force lines that touch suppliers beyond the missile makers: $13.8 billion for Space Force procurement, $2 billion toward a space-based moving target indicator capability and $4.5 billion for the Sentinel ICBM, which the committee says supports the President's request. Those lines are smaller than the munitions block but broaden who benefits if the bill survives.

## Ships are funded in pieces

The shipbuilding section is where the trade-off appears. The $42 billion includes $1.9 billion in additional money for a second DDG 51 destroyer, $329 million for the submarine industrial base and $595 million for the surface ship industrial base. But the bill "provides incremental funding" for one Columbia-class submarine, two Virginia-class submarines, one carrier refueling overhaul, one FF(X) frigate, one LPD Flight II and one LHA replacement ship. The committee's stated reason is "more effective use of funds for multiyear munitions and other higher priority investments in fiscal year 2027."

In plain terms, appropriators chose to buy missiles in full and pay for some ships in installments. That does not cancel those ships, but it defers cash that shipbuilders such as General Dynamics (GD) and Huntington Ingalls (HII) would otherwise receive sooner, and it leaves the later years of each program more exposed to the next budget fight.

How big a cut this is depends on the baseline, and the reports do not agree. USNI News headlined its coverage a [$20 billion cut](https://news.usni.org/2026/11/18/senate-appropriators-cut-20b-from-navy-shipbuilding-request) to the Navy's request, while [MLQ.ai](https://mlq.ai/news/senate-appropriators-would-cut-about-18-billion-from-navy-shipbuilding-and-zero-out-the-trump-class-battleships-first-funds/) puts it at about $18 billion, from a $60.1 billion request. MLQ.ai also reports that the committee zeroed out a $1 billion long-lead request for the first Trump-class battleship. I could not read the USNI article itself, so treat the exact cut as unsettled until the Navy's own comparison tables appear.

## What to watch

**The House side.** This is the Senate's version. The final bill has to reconcile with the House, and the shipbuilding increments and the battleship decision are natural places for disagreement.

**Dec. 11.** The current stopgap runs out then. Until a full-year bill passes, the new multiyear munitions contracts cannot start, so the revenue timing for the nine missiles is later than the headline suggests.

**The reconciliation package.** The administration's April request sought $1.5 trillion for the Pentagon, with $350 billion of it through reconciliation, per ExecutiveGov. The Senate bill absorbs about $69 billion of the mandatory portion, but if the larger package stalls, Pentagon technology chief Emil Michael said the department would consider cutting legacy weapons to protect drone funding, ExecutiveGov reports. That is a signal that some older programs could lose out.

**Contract awards.** An appropriation is permission to spend, not a purchase order. Watch for multiyear award announcements on the nine named munitions, because those announcements, not the bill, determine which companies book the orders.
