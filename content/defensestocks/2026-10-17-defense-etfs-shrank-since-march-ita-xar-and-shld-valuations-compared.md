## What the issuer pages show today

Defense ETFs were one of the busiest corners of the market in 2025 and early 2026. They are now a more sober story. Using each issuer's own fund page, the three largest pure-play products look like this.

| Fund | Net assets | Expense ratio | Holdings | Return since Jan. 1 | 1-year return |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| iShares U.S. Aerospace & Defense ETF (ITA) | $12.09B (Oct. 16) | 0.37% | 50 | -3.25% | -0.80% |
| State Street SPDR S&P Aerospace & Defense ETF (XAR) | $5.36B (Oct. 15) | 0.35% | 50 | -4.03% | -1.59% |
| Global X Defense Tech ETF (SHLD) | $6.35B (Oct. 15) | 0.50% | 50 | not shown | -13.45% |

Return figures are NAV total returns as of Sept. 30, 2026, per [iShares](https://www.ishares.com/us/products/239502/ishares-us-aerospace-defense-etf), [State Street](https://www.ssga.com/us/en/intermediary/etfs/state-street-spdr-sp-aerospace-defense-etf-xar) and [Global X](https://www.globalxetfs.com/funds/shld/). Net assets and holdings counts are the pages' latest as-of dates.

Two things stand out. First, the sector is roughly flat to slightly negative this year, which is a sharp contrast to the "boom" language that surrounded these funds in the spring. Second, SHLD, the most heavily "tech" slanted of the three, has fared noticeably worse than the plain aerospace and defense funds over a year.

## Assets are lower than in March

In a [March 2 ETF Action survey](https://www.etfaction.com/inside-the-defense-etf-boom-visualizing-holdings-overlap-and-valuations/), the same three funds held the following net assets: ITA $15.93B, SHLD $7.40B and XAR $6.12B. Today's issuer figures are $12.09B, $6.35B and $5.36B, respectively.

That is a decline of roughly a quarter at ITA, 14% at SHLD and about 12% at XAR, by simple subtraction of the two sets of figures. The comparison needs a caveat: fund assets change with both share prices and investor flows, and the two sources were collected on different dates and by different methods. The pages above do not split the change between the two, so this should not be read as proof of outflows. It does show that the pool of money in these products is smaller than it was seven months ago.

ETF Action also noted at the time that the category had 25 defense ETFs, that 11 of them had launched since the start of 2025, and that the group took in over $15 billion of new money over that period. Its central observation was that returns had been strong but earnings growth had not kept up, so valuation multiples had expanded almost as fast as share prices. The year-to-date numbers above suggest some of that has since reversed.

## Why the P/E figures do not line up

The issuers report valuation on different bases, and that matters when comparing.

- ITA shows a P/E of 34.20 and a price-to-book of 5.74. The page does not label the basis in the data we could read, so treat it as a headline figure, not a forward estimate.
- XAR shows a P/E of 25.75 on a fiscal-year-one (forward) basis as of Oct. 15. State Street lists the underlying index's trailing P/E separately, at 31.45.
- SHLD's page lists 28.88 for 2025 and 23.42 for 2026, which implies the market expects earnings growth in the funds' holdings.

The honest read is that a quick glance at 34 versus 26 versus 23 overstates the gap. XAR's trailing index figure of 31.45 sits much closer to ITA's, and SHLD's 2026 number is the only one of the three that is clearly forward-looking and labeled that way. Before concluding that one fund is cheaper, check that you are comparing like with like.

## Same sector, very different portfolios

The structural differences explain more of the performance gap than the P/E numbers do.

**XAR is the equal-weight option.** Its ten largest positions are all between 2.96% and 3.33%, from Honeywell Aerospace through Lockheed Martin (LMT), TransDigm (TDG), Moog (MOG.A), HEICO (HEI), Woodward (WWD), Northrop Grumman (NOC), L3Harris (LHX), GE Aerospace (GE) and General Dynamics (GD), per State Street. A mid-sized supplier gets about the same weight as a prime. That gives it more exposure to the commercial aerospace supply chain than the headline name suggests.

**ITA is market-cap weighted.** In ETF Action's March table its weighted-average market cap was $166.7B against $60.5B for XAR, and its top ten holdings were 75.7% of the fund against 38.6% for XAR. Larger companies dominate, so it behaves more like a bet on a handful of the largest names.

**SHLD is a different animal.** Its top three holdings are Palantir Technologies (PLTR) at 12.57%, RTX Corporation (RTX) at 9.07% and Lockheed Martin (LMT) at 8.57%, which is about 30% of the fund in three stocks, according to Global X. It is also the most global of the group: 62.94% of assets are in U.S. companies as of Sept. 30, with the rest spread across South Korea (6.98%), Britain (6.18%), Germany (4.90%), France (4.67%), Sweden (3.60%) and others. Software-heavy Palantir and European and Asian rearmament plays make it a very different exposure from XAR's supplier-heavy list.

## What it means for portfolio construction

Without making any recommendation, there are a few things worth weighing when deciding how a defense sleeve is built.

1. **Overlap is high at the top.** Lockheed Martin appears in the top holdings of both XAR and SHLD. Holding more than one fund can concentrate rather than diversify.
2. **Fees are close, except for SHLD.** The gap between 0.35% and 0.37% is small. SHLD's 0.50% is higher, which is a cost on a fund that has also lagged over a year.
3. **The thing you own is not only defense.** XAR's holdings include commercial aerospace names like TransDigm, HEICO and Woodward, so its results depend as much on airline build rates as on munitions budgets. SHLD's depend on Palantir's share price.
4. **Geography matters.** SHLD's roughly 37% non-U.S. exposure ties it to European budgets and currencies in a way ITA and XAR are not.

## What to watch next

The next test is earnings. Many of the primes held in these funds report this month, and if backlogs convert to revenue faster than expected, forward P/Es like XAR's 25.75 and SHLD's 23.42 would look easier to defend. If not, the multiples that expanded last year remain the exposed part of the sector.

The other measure to track is asset trends. If the next set of issuer figures shows assets rising while returns are flat, that points to dip-buying; if both fall together, the pullback is still working through. Either way, the sector's fund count has grown quickly, and a crowded product field tends to thin out when performance disappoints.
