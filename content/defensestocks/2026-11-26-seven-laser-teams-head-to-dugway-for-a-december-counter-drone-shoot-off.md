## What happened

The Pentagon has named the seven teams that will fire lasers at drones in December. According to a [Nov. 16 Department of War release](https://dronexl.co/2026/11/16/pentagon-seven-laser-teams-dugway-counter-drone-shoot-off/), the teams were picked after a "comprehensive competitive review" of 13 technical white paper submissions. [DefenseScoop](https://defensescoop.com/2026/11/16/counter-drone-high-energy-laser-shoot-off/) reports the selections were announced by the Army-led Joint Interagency Task Force 401 (JIATF 401), which helps the military buy counter-drone technology, together with the Pentagon's research and engineering office.

The seven entrants, with their systems:

- Allen Control Systems: Bullfrog HEL
- Aurelius Systems: Archimedes
- AeroVironment (AVAV), through its BlueHalo unit: LOCUST X1
- Kord Technologies: Firefly
- Lockheed Martin (LMT), with IPG Photonics (IPGP) lasers: Crossbow
- Thor Dynamics: Laser Armor
- Yrikka: TALOS, a drone-based laser

The schedule is tight. Vendors spend November completing laser safety certifications and command-and-control integrations. Candidate systems then arrive at Dugway Proving Ground in Utah for about a week of safety reviews, communications checks and range-readiness testing. Live-fire vignettes against representative unmanned aerial targets begin by mid-December, judging tracking and defeat "both above and below the horizon," the release says.

## Why the shoot-off matters

The stated payoff is speed. Army Brig. Gen. Matthew Ross, who directs JIATF 401, said the event is built so that "systems that perform effectively or successfully can immediately result in a purchase order." In an [August DefenseScoop report](https://defensescoop.com/2026/08/21/us-military-directed-energy-shoot-off-counter-drone/), Ross said a successful result "allows us to start building the production lines," and that he did not want to down-select to "one or two systems."

That is unusual for directed energy, a field long run as science-and-technology projects. Assistant Army Secretary Brent Ingraham made the point in the Army's [Sept. 2 announcement](https://www.army.mil/article/295045) of its first production contract for a high-energy laser: "Directed energy weapons have been considered S&T projects." Emil Michael, the Pentagon's research and engineering chief, said in the shoot-off release that "Scaled Directed Energy is one of the Department's six critical technology areas."

The shoot-off also sits beside a separate pilot. [Air & Space Forces Magazine](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/counter-drone-task-force-directed-energy-systems-shoot-off/) reports that the pilot is a congressional requirement in the 2026 National Defense Authorization Act and covers four systems: AV's palletized laser, Epirus's Leonidas microwave, Kord's Firefly (scalable from 5 to 30 kilowatts) and Boeing's Compact Laser Weapon. The shoot-off lets the task force look at additional vendors.

The same report says JIATF 401 chose Grand Forks Air Force Base, Whiteman Air Force Base, Fort Huachuca, Fort Bliss and Naval Base Kitsap as pilot test sites. Ross described the aim as testing these tools in an operational environment, and said directed energy "gives commanders another option within a layered defense." In other words, the Pentagon is not betting on lasers alone. It wants them as one layer next to microwaves, missiles and guns, and the shoot-off is the cheapest way to learn which lasers deserve a production line. The first venue for the event was also moved: DefenseScoop says it was first planned for Yuma Proving Ground in Arizona before being shifted to Dugway.

## AV already has the production contract

AV is the only entrant with a production program behind it. The Army's Sept. 2 release describes an other-transaction production agreement for the Enduring-High Energy Laser system but gives no dollar value. [Defense News and Military Times](https://militarytimes.com/industry/techwatch/2026/09/03/us-army-launches-laser-production-with-465m-contract-award/) put it at $464.8 million, citing AV's release, for "dozens" of 30-kilowatt LOCUST X3 lasers. DefenseScoop rounds the award to "nearly $500 million," so treat the exact figure with a little care. Military Times says the X3 is aimed at small drones in Defense Department groups one through three.

The shoot-off entry is a different system, the LOCUST X1. So AV is effectively competing twice: its X3 is already the Army's pick for the production line, while the X1 must prove itself against rivals. Kord's Firefly and AV's laser both appear in the earlier pilot as well, so the task force is watching them in more than one setting.

## IPG shows how small the laser line still is

The most useful financial read-through comes from IPG Photonics, which supplies the laser inside Lockheed's Crossbow. In a [Feb. 26 release](https://www.ipgphotonics.com/newsroom/news/IPG-Photonics-Receives-Order-From-Lockheed-Martin), IPG announced a follow-on Crossbow order from Lockheed worth approximately $10 million, with deliveries expected over several quarters.

IPG's [third-quarter results](https://finviz.com/news/414198/ipg-photonics-announces-third-quarter-2026-financial-results), released Nov. 3, show revenue of $287.5 million, up 15% from $250.8 million a year earlier. Advanced Solutions, the segment that includes defense, brought in $48.0 million, up 33% from $36.1 million, on "higher sales in semiconductor and defense applications." The release does not break out defense, and it does not mention Crossbow or Lockheed. Against the quarter's total, a roughly $10 million order is a small line.

[Optics.org](https://www.optics.org/news/laser-weapons-and-battery-welding-aid-ipg-photonics-sales-growth) reported that most Crossbow shipments to Lockheed are complete, with the remainder due before year-end. CEO Mark Gitin told listeners that sales will likely be "lumpy" in the near term because the market is still at an early stage, and said there is strong customer interest in the new 8-kilowatt Crossbow SE1. IPG guided fourth-quarter revenue to $265 million to $295 million. Crossbow itself is designed for group one and group two drones, per [optics.org's 2025 coverage](https://optics.org/news/ipg-ships-counter-drone-laser-weapons-to-lockheed-martin), the smallest classes. That matters because the production laser the Army chose targets up to group three.

## What to watch

First, whether the shoot-off produces purchase orders at all. Ross's framing leaves room for several winners, and none of the sources gives a budget, a quantity or a cost per shot for the December event. Without those, the promise of "an immediate purchase order" is a process claim, not a revenue forecast.

Second, how the entrants differ on power and range. The shoot-off tests defeat "above and below the horizon," and the systems range from small, few-kilowatt units to 30-kilowatt designs. Results that separate vehicle-mounted lasers from drone-based or compact ones would show where the Army wants to spend.

Third, timing. Live fire begins "by mid-December," and the release gives no date for results or orders. For investors tracking AVAV, LMT and IPGP, the signal to look for is any follow-on order language in company disclosures after the event, not the demonstration footage. Directed energy is moving from prototype to production, but one contract so far, and one roughly $10 million Crossbow order, are the extent of what is publicly priced.
