## What the annual report says

Most defense ETF talk in the U.S. centers on iShares U.S. Aerospace & Defense (ITA), SPDR S&P Aerospace & Defense (XAR) and thematic funds. A smaller product answers a different question: what happens if you hold only European companies? The Select STOXX Europe Aerospace & Defense ETF (EUAD) filed its annual shareholder report with the SEC for the year that ended Aug. 31, 2026, and the numbers are worth a slow read.

According to the [filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001484018/000199937126024910/euad-ncsr_083126.htm), the fund "rose 6.36%, while the S&P 500 Index advanced 20.38%." Its own benchmark, the STOXX Europe TMI Aerospace & Defense index, returned 10.85% over the same year. So the fund trailed its index as well as the broad U.S. market. The report lists net assets of $1,136,003,447, an expense ratio of 0.50% and 31 holdings.

That is a fiscal-year figure, not a calendar-year one, and it ended almost three months ago. It says nothing about where the fund trades today. It does show how one European defense basket behaved during a year when the sector's loudest story was European rearmament.

## One stock did the damage

The report's explanation is blunt. Rheinmetall (RNMBY, the U.S. over-the-counter ticker the filing uses) "declined -32.01%, correcting from peak valuations reached in late September 2025," and the filing calls the company, "the prior year's standout performer," the "primary drag on returns." Elsewhere the report says valuation multiples "contracted sharply" after that peak.

Read that carefully. The filing does not say Rheinmetall's business weakened in the period. It says the multiple investors were willing to pay for it fell. That is a valuation story, and it is the same risk U.S. holders face when a defense name trades far above its peers.

The fund's holdings list also shows why one stock did not decide everything. Rheinmetall sat at 1.7% of net assets at fiscal year-end, according to the same report, below MTU Aero Engines at 4.6% and Airbus (EADSY) at 4.5%. The report presents the Rheinmetall decline as the main drag even at that modest size.

## The best performers were not tanks or shells

The report names Rolls-Royce Holdings (RYCEY) as the fund's strongest contributor at +41.71%, and credits higher engine flying hours, aftermarket revenue and new defense contract wins. Safran (SAFRY) rose 18.86% in the same discussion, per the filing. Saab (SAABY) gained 17%, which the filing ties to "strengthening Nordic and European defense procurement."

The fund's industry split makes the point clearer. The filing shows Defense at 54.8% of investments, Aircraft & Parts at 39.8% and Transport Operations & Services at 5.4%. A European "defense" ETF is, in effect, close to 40% civil and military aerospace supply chain. When airlines fly more hours, the engine makers behind them earn aftermarket revenue, and that helped the fund even as the pure-play rearmament names swung.

For investors comparing funds, this is the main takeaway. Two ETFs that both carry a defense label can be driven by different forces: one by order books and government budgets, the other by flight hours and engine servicing.

## How the U.S. funds compare today

The U.S. products offer a useful contrast, with a caution on dates. EUAD's figures end Aug. 31. The U.S. fund pages below were read on Nov. 19-20, and their return figures are for different periods, so none of this is a like-for-like ranking.

ITA's [iShares page](https://www.ishares.com/us/products/239502/ishares-us-aerospace-defense-etf) shows a P/E of 34.85 and a P/B of 5.90 as of Nov. 19, 2026, with 50 holdings, a 0.37% expense ratio and a year-to-date return of -4.34%. XAR's [SSGA page](https://www.ssga.com/us/en/intermediary/etfs/spdr-sp-aerospace-defense-etf-xar) lists a P/B of 3.57 and a forward (FY1) P/E of 24.33 as of Nov. 19, also with 50 holdings. It shows a one-year NAV return of 26.13% and a year-to-date return of -5.50%, both measured at the end of October.

Two points follow. First, the U.S. funds' year-to-date returns are negative, so the 20.38% S&P 500 figure in EUAD's report should not be read as a defense-sector benchmark; it measures the broad market over a different window. Second, ITA's P/B sits well above XAR's. XAR's top ten holdings sit in a narrow band of 3.22% to 3.53% each, according to its page, so no single name dominates; the pages give no fuller explanation of the valuation gap.

The EUAD annual report does not give the same valuation measures, and sites that carry its holdings disagree on the number of positions, so this post does not quote a P/E for it. That is itself a point for readers: a European fund can be harder to value from public documents than a U.S. one.

## What to watch

Three habits are worth building from this report, whichever fund a reader follows. Check the fiscal-year end before comparing returns. Read the holdings list before trusting the label. Ask whether a fall came from earnings or from the multiple.

- **The next EUAD report.** The fund's next shareholder report will show whether the Rheinmetall drag persisted or reversed, and whether Rolls-Royce's aftermarket tailwind held.
- **Multiples, not just orders.** The filing's own explanation for the year was multiple compression. Backlog figures from European contractors matter less to a fund's return if the price paid for each euro of earnings keeps shrinking.
- **Composition before labels.** Before comparing a European defense fund with a U.S. one, check how much sits in civil aerospace. In EUAD's case the report puts it near 40% of investments.
- **Fiscal-year dates.** Funds report on different calendars. A 6.36% return to Aug. 31 and a 26.13% return to Oct. 31 are not comparable, and the U.S. funds' year-to-date declines show how fast the picture changes.

None of this is a recommendation on any fund or stock. It is a reminder that a European defense ETF's year came down to a small number of valuation swings and an engine-maker's rally, and that the label on a fund says less than the holdings list does.
