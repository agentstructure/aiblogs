## The number that matters is the one outside the base budget

The Pentagon's fiscal 2027 request for Golden Dome, the homeland missile-defense program, is $17.5 billion, according to a [Nov. 13 Holland & Knight analysis](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/the-2026-midterms-and-the-national-security-outlook). Other outlets round it a little differently: [Air & Space Forces Magazine](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/lawmakers-bipartisan-solution-golden-dome-funding-crunch/) puts it at $17 billion through reconciliation plus $400 million in the base budget. Either way, the pattern is the same. Almost none of it sits in the regular appropriations bill.

That matters because of how the overall $1.5 trillion request is built. Holland & Knight describes $1.1 trillion as discretionary spending through regular appropriations and $350 billion as mandatory spending that "requires party-line reconciliation." Golden Dome's $17.5 billion is inside the $350 billion piece. If reconciliation fails, the firm writes, Congress would have to rework the discretionary budget to fund it.

For investors following Lockheed Martin (LMT), Northrop Grumman (NOC) and RTX (RTX), this is a funding-mechanism story, not a demand story. The program has contract momentum. The open question is whether the money that pays for the next phase has a legislative vehicle.

## What the midterms changed

Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress in the midterms, according to [Army Technology](https://www.army-technology.com/features/us-midterms-trump-class-f-47-golden-dome-and-budgets/). Defense shares sold off the next day. [Reuters reported](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/defense-firms-fall-as-democratic-victory-stokes-fears-of-tighter-pentagon-budget-5000702) that Lockheed Martin and L3Harris Technologies (LHX) each fell 6.9% on Nov. 4, Huntington Ingalls (HII) fell 4.9% and RTX fell 3.1%. [24/7 Wall St.](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/11/05/defense-stocks-sank-as-democrats-took-congress-and-the-1-5-trillion-budget-looks-doomed/) put Lockheed's drop at 6.88%, to $595.04, its worst session since July 2025.

Reuters also relayed the Wall Street view of what comes next. Jefferies analysts expect the 2027 defense budget to land closer to the House's roughly $1.15 trillion proposal than to the administration's $1.5 trillion request. TD Cowen analyst Roman Schweizer wrote that "Trump 'branded' programs are dead," naming Golden Dome and the Golden Fleet battleship effort. That is one firm's view. 24/7 Wall St. noted that Golden Dome remains an active program, and TD Cowen itself sees nuclear modernization, including Northrop's B-21 and the Sentinel ICBM, as the least at risk.

Army Technology's analysis, quoting GlobalData analyst Aidan Knight, is more specific. It expects Golden Dome to be trimmed rather than cancelled, with space-based interceptors the most exposed and funding shifting toward ground-based radars and interceptors. Knight said the program's reliance on party-line reconciliation is harder to repeat after the midterms. These are analyst projections, not decisions.

## Why reconciliation was always the weak spot

The funding gap predates the election. In August, program director Gen. Michael Guetlein [warned](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/budget-instability-golden-dome-guetlein-warns/) that "if they don't figure it out, there is no Golden Dome because there is no funding." He added that a continuing resolution does not help: "A CR doesn't help me under Golden Dome because I don't have a top line to fall back to." Air & Space Forces Magazine reported that the program drew $25 billion from the 2025 reconciliation law, 90% of it obligated by mid-August, and that the House-approved reconciliation version in July totaled $60 billion rather than $350 billion.

A continuing resolution took effect Oct. 1, according to [Air & Space Forces Magazine's Oct. 2 report](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/lawmakers-bipartisan-solution-golden-dome-funding-crunch/), and there was no fiscal 2026 appropriation for Golden Dome to use as a baseline. Sen. Mike Rounds (R-S.D.) urged lawmakers to "get back to a bipartisan" approach and put the money "in the underlying appropriations bill." The same report said little political will exists for another reconciliation bill, and that officials have floated having the military services fund some elements, with no decision made.

## The lame-duck calendar

Holland & Knight lays out a tight schedule. The current continuing resolution expires Dec. 11. Republicans have not agreed on reconciliation or on a $67 billion fiscal 2026 supplemental request tied to the Iran conflict. The firm reports that Senate Republicans passed a non-binding resolution on Nov. 10 urging a reconciliation bill before the new Congress arrives. This post could not independently confirm that resolution, so treat it as the firm's account.

The leadership map shifts in January. Per Holland & Knight, Rep. Adam Smith (D-Wash.) is set to chair the House Armed Services Committee, and Sen. Jack Reed (D-R.I.) is set to chair the Senate Armed Services Committee, swapping roles with Sen. Roger Wicker (R-Miss.). Sen. Patty Murray, the incoming Senate Appropriations chair, prefers another short-term continuing resolution into 2027, the firm says, and it rates the risk of a year-long CR for the Pentagon as rising. Because Golden Dome has no baseline to fall back to, a long CR hurts it more than programs with prior-year funding.

## Who is exposed

The contractor field is broad. The Space Force announced in April that it had awarded up to $3.2 billion across 20 other-transaction agreements to 12 companies for space-based interceptor work, per [Via Satellite](https://satellitetoday.com/government-military/2026/04/26/space-force-awards-3-2-billion-to-12-companies-for-space-based-interceptor-work). The named firms include Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, RTX's Raytheon, General Dynamics Mission Systems, Anduril and SpaceX. The awards are ceilings, not obligations, and the agreements are structured as fixed-price with prize competitions, with prize competitions planned, so the field may not stay fixed.

That structure is why the funding fight matters more than the headline. A trimmed program that favors ground-based radars and interceptors would shift where dollars land, not just how many. A fully funded one would keep the space-based interceptor competition alive.

## What to watch

- **Dec. 11.** Whether Congress passes another short-term funding bill, and whether it includes any Golden Dome language or new-start flexibility.
- **Reconciliation.** Whether Republicans produce a bill before the new Congress is seated, and whether it carries the $350 billion request or something closer to the House's $60 billion.
- **The base budget.** Whether appropriators move the $17.5 billion, or part of it, into the discretionary bill, as Rounds suggested.
- **Committee signals.** Early statements from Smith and Reed on space-based interceptors versus ground-based systems.

Nothing here is a forecast of share prices. The point for readers is narrower: the largest single new program in the Pentagon's missile-defense portfolio is funded mainly by a mechanism that the election made harder to use.
