## The budgets are real, the share prices are not following

European defense is the clearest case in the sector of spending growing faster than equity returns. According to SIPRI figures [reported by Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/04/buoyed-by-european-rearmament-boost-global-military-spending-jumps-to-2-89-trillion/), global military spending reached $2.89 trillion in 2025, an eleventh straight annual rise. Europe spent $864 billion, up 14%, and SIPRI called the continent the main driver of the global increase. US spending, by contrast, fell 7.5%, mostly because no new Ukraine aid packages were approved.

NATO's own numbers point the same way. Secretary General Mark Rutte said Europe and Canada added $139 billion in core defense spending over the past year, a 20% increase, [according to Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/07/06/nato-leaders-to-affirm-record-defence-spending-as-us-plans-european-drawdown). Allies have committed to 5% of GDP on defense by 2035. [XTB's breakdown](https://www.xtb.com/int/market-analysis/news-and-research/european-defence-stocks-in-panic-what-s-next-for-europe-s-rearmament-trade) splits that into 3.5% for conventional military spending and 1.5% for infrastructure, logistics, cybersecurity and resilience.

Yet the equities that were the face of the trade have given back a great deal. That gap between budget direction and share price is the story for European and allied defense investors this autumn.

## Rheinmetall: a record backlog and a halved share price

Rheinmetall (RHM) is the sharpest example. [Coverage from late August](https://www.ad-hoc-news.de/boerse/news/corporate-news/rheinmetall-stock-softens-as-record-80-5-billion-backlog-meets-trimmed/69994706) put its order backlog at a record €80.5 billion at the end of the first half, and reported that 2026 revenue guidance had been trimmed to €13.7 to €14.2 billion from €14.0 to €14.5 billion. First-half revenue was €5.2 billion with an operating result of €786 million, roughly a 15% margin, according to [a mid-September report](https://www.ad-hoc-news.de/boerse/news/corporate-news/rheinmetall-stock-falls-as-guidance-cut-and-jpmorgan-warning-weigh-on/70095567) that also cites a full-year margin target of about 19%.

The share price has not rewarded the backlog. That mid-September report put the stock near €993, about 51% below its 52-week high of €2,007 set in early October 2025, and down about 36% year to date. It also noted that JPMorgan placed the shares on a Negative Catalyst Watch in early September, while Deutsche Bank kept a Buy rating with a €1,800 target. Analysts are plainly split, which is itself informative: the debate is about timing and valuation, not about whether demand exists.

Two things drove the reset. First, [Germany cancelled the F126 frigate program](https://www.xtb.com/int/market-analysis/news-and-research/european-defence-stocks-in-panic-what-s-next-for-europe-s-rearmament-trade), a contract of more than €12 billion for six ships, and chose eight smaller MEKO A-200 frigates from TKMS instead. XTB reported that Rheinmetall, Hensoldt (HAG) and Renk (R3NK) fell sharply on the news, with Rheinmetall losing more than €10 billion of market value even though the cancelled contract was worth considerably less. Second, XTB noted a valuation question: even after the decline, Rheinmetall carried a price-to-earnings ratio of around 60 at the time of writing.

## Why a backlog is not the same as earnings

For investors, the useful distinction is between order intake and conversion. A backlog of €80.5 billion is a multi-year queue, and its value depends on how fast factories can turn orders into revenue at acceptable margins. Guidance trimmed by a few hundred million euros, in a year when revenue is expected to be around €14 billion, is a reminder that delivery schedules slip and program mix shifts.

The Euronews piece captured a related point from Fabrice Pothier of Rasmussen Global: money is not the main problem, and industry and governments must take more risks and produce systems faster. That is a statement about execution, and execution is what separates suppliers with capacity from those with only order books.

Cost per intercept is the other pressure. Former US NATO ambassador Kurt Volker told Euronews that firing roughly $1 million Patriot missiles at drones costing around $30,000 is unsustainable, and he pointed to Ukraine's low-cost counter-drone systems as a model. Procurement is likely to tilt toward cheaper air defense and drone countermeasures, which favors a different set of suppliers than the traditional large-platform winners.

## The political backdrop is shifting under the trade

Three developments matter for the demand outlook.

**US drawdown.** Euronews reported that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced a review of US troop levels in Europe, where about 80,000 US troops are stationed, and that the Pentagon has cut some capabilities it contributes to NATO in wartime. The administration's "NATO 3.0" framing envisions a more European-led alliance. That strengthens the structural case for European procurement, since allies would have to fill the gaps themselves.

**Joint programs.** The Council of the EU announced five European Defense Projects of Common Interest on September 28, [BT News reported](https://breakthroughnews.org/2026/09/30/nato-chief-says-no-imminent-danger-but-rearmament-of-europe-continues-in-full-swing/), including DECODER and Eastern Flank Watch, with Ukraine listed as a partner in most. Joint funding of this kind could channel money to cross-border consortia rather than to a single national champion.

**Domestic pushback.** The same report described protests against austerity budgets, including demonstrations over France's proposed 2027 budget and German student strikes against conscription. A coalition called Stop ReArm Europe cites EU Defense Commissioner Andrius Kubilius's estimate of €7 trillion in military spending by 2035. Rutte, for his part, said there is "no imminent danger" to alliance territory. Public support for spending is not guaranteed to hold through budget fights, and XTB noted that investors may be pricing in political shifts in Italy, the UK and Germany, while adding there is little evidence of an actual cut so far.

## What to watch next

- **Third-quarter results.** Rheinmetall and its European peers report in the coming weeks. The key lines are backlog conversion, margin against the roughly 19% full-year target, and whether guidance moves again.
- **Program decisions.** The F126 episode shows a single procurement choice can reprice a stock. Watch national decisions on frigates, armored vehicles and air defense for the next such swing.
- **Budget season.** Autumn 2027 budget drafts in France, Germany and elsewhere will test whether the 5% pledge turns into appropriations.
- **Cost-efficient systems.** Contracts for counter-drone and lower-cost interceptors will indicate where procurement priorities are heading.

The central tension is straightforward. European governments have set a multi-year spending path and the order books reflect it, but the market is now asking harder questions about delivery, valuation and political durability. For investors following the sector, the gap between record backlogs and a share price roughly half its peak is where the analysis should focus.
