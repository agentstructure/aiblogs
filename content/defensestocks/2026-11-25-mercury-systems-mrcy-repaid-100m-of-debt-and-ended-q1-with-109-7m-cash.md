## What happened

Mercury Systems (MRCY), which sells signal and data processing electronics into aerospace and defense programs, reported fiscal first-quarter 2027 results on Nov. 10 for the quarter ended Oct. 2. The income statement looked strong. The balance sheet moved a lot more than most coverage suggested.

According to the [company's earnings release](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001049521/000104952126000096/a2027q1earningsreleaseex.htm), revenue was $253.2 million, against $225.2 million in the same quarter a year earlier. GAAP net income was $13.1 million, versus a net loss of $12.5 million a year ago. Bookings were $370 million, a book-to-bill ratio of 1.46. Total backlog was approaching $2.1 billion, about $637 million more than a year earlier, and over $1.0 billion of it is expected to be recognized as revenue within the next 12 months.

The cash lines tell a different story. Cash and cash equivalents were $109.7 million on Oct. 2, down from $214.3 million on July 3. Long-term debt fell to $341.5 million from $441.5 million over the same period. The cash flow statement shows a $100 million line labeled "Payments under credit facilities" in the quarter, and none in the prior-year quarter.

## Where the cash went

Two things pulled cash down at once. The first was a deliberate choice: $100 million went to the credit facilities. That alone accounts for most of the roughly $104.6 million decline in the cash balance, though the release does not itself present that subtraction, so treat the arithmetic as ours.

The second was operations. Operating cash flow was negative $14.0 million, compared with positive $2.2 million in the prior-year quarter. Capital spending, which Mercury defines to include capitalized software development, was $10.4 million versus $6.5 million. Mercury defines free cash flow as operating cash flow less that capital spending, and on that basis free cash flow was negative $24.4 million, compared with negative $4.4 million a year earlier.

Management's own words in the [release](https://finviz.com/news/420460/mercury-systems-reports-first-quarter-results) were brief. CEO Bill Ballhaus listed the cash flow used in operations, the free cash outflow and a net debt figure of $232 million, "down 19% year-over-year," but the release carries no explanation of the outflow and no commentary from the finance chief on cash, inventory or debt.

## Inventory is the visible culprit

The balance sheet offers a likely clue. Inventory was $398.6 million on Oct. 2, up from $367.0 million on July 3, a rise of about $31.6 million by our subtraction. The release gives no reason for the increase.

An [Earnings Whispers analysis](https://finviz.com/news/420748/mercury-systems-crushes-estimates-and-raises-fy27-outlook-but-margin-peak-and-cash-burn-temper-a-record-quarter) of the quarter attributes the free cash outflow to a roughly $32 million inventory build and says net working capital rose $33 million year over year. Those are the analysis's characterizations, not company statements in the release, and we could not confirm them against a primary document that explains the build.

For a supplier, an inventory build is not automatically a warning. A company with bookings up 48% and a larger near-term backlog needs parts on the shelf to ship against it. But it is also exactly the kind of cash absorption that can persist if deliveries slip, and it is the number that connects strong orders to weaker cash.

## Profit rose faster than sales

Margins widened sharply. Adjusted EBITDA was $56.4 million, up from $35.6 million a year ago, according to the release. The Earnings Whispers piece puts the adjusted EBITDA margin at 22.3% versus 15.9% a year earlier, and gross margin at 35.2%. It also says management attributed part of the first-quarter margin strength to favorable mix that it expects to normalize.

The release's reconciliation shows how much of adjusted EBITDA is added back. From GAAP net income of $13.1 million, Mercury adds interest expense of $4.1 million, depreciation of $8.5 million, amortization of intangibles of $9.3 million, stock-based and other non-cash compensation of $17.2 million, and about $4.0 million of other items, including restructuring of $2.0 million. Adjusted EBITDA is a company-defined measure, and the gap between it and the $13.1 million GAAP profit is a reminder to read cash flow alongside it.

Adjusted earnings per share were $0.52 versus $0.26, and GAAP diluted earnings per share were $0.21 versus a loss of $0.21.

## The outlook is raised, but the release gives no numbers

Ballhaus said in the release that "we are raising our outlook for fiscal year 2027." The release gives no figures, and it does not state the prior outlook, so this post will not characterize the size of the change.

The Earnings Whispers analysis reports specifics that it attributes to management: double-digit revenue growth with sales approaching $1.1 billion, adjusted EBITDA of roughly $210 million, and free cash flow conversion approaching 40%. These come from a single secondhand source, and the release itself does not carry them, so readers should check them against the company's earnings call or presentation. The same analysis says management excluded a Palantir automation partnership and prime-contractor framework flowdowns from the outlook. We could not verify that from a primary document.

If the conversion target holds, the first quarter's negative free cash flow would need to reverse over the remaining three quarters. That makes cash the cleanest test of this year's plan.

## What to watch next

- **Inventory and unbilled work.** Does the roughly $399 million of inventory convert to shipments in the second quarter, or keep growing? The next quarterly balance sheet will show it.
- **Cash after the paydown.** With $109.7 million of cash and $341.5 million of long-term debt, the company has reduced leverage but has less liquidity than it did in July. Operating cash flow in the next two quarters shows whether it needs to draw on the credit facilities again.
- **Margin durability.** If favorable mix fades as management expects, adjusted EBITDA margin should come down from 22.3%. How far is the open question.
- **Backlog conversion.** More than $1.0 billion of backlog is expected to turn into revenue within 12 months. The rate of conversion matters more than the backlog total.
- **Funding backdrop.** Defense spending bills and continuing-resolution timing can move the timing of customer orders, which feeds directly into a supplier's inventory planning.

## Why it matters

Mercury is a useful read on the electronics layer beneath missile, radar and space programs: companies that sell to the primes and live on conversion of orders into cash. This quarter shows both halves of that business. Orders, profit and backlog rose, while cash on hand fell by about half and free cash flow moved the wrong way.

None of that is a verdict on the stock. It does mean the figure to follow in the next report is not backlog, which the company has already shown growing, but whether operating cash flow turns positive as inventory is worked down. This post is information and commentary only, not investment advice.
