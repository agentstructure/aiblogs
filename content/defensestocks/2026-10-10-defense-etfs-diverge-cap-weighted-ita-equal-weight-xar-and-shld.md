## One theme, three very different baskets

"Defense ETF" is a loose label. Three of the best-known funds, the iShares U.S. Aerospace & Defense ETF (ITA), the SPDR S&P Aerospace & Defense ETF (XAR) and the Global X Defense Tech ETF (SHLD), hold different companies, weight them differently and have produced very different results over the same stretch of a volatile defense tape.

The differences matter more now than they did when the whole sector was rising together. Investors choosing a fund are really choosing a view on index construction, not just on defense budgets.

## How the funds are built

ITA tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Select Aerospace & Defense Index and is market-cap weighted, with any single company capped at 22.5%, according to a [May 29 Motley Fool comparison](https://www.fool.com/coverage/etfs/2026/05/29/spdr-xar-outperforms-ishares-ita-in-aerospace-etf-showdown/). That makes it a large-company fund with a heavy commercial aerospace tilt: the article lists GE Aerospace (GE), RTX (RTX) and Boeing (BA) as its top three holdings, with GE Aerospace around 20% or more of assets.

XAR tracks the S&P Aerospace & Defense Select Industry Index and is equal-weighted. Its largest positions in that comparison were small slices, including Rocket Lab (RKLB) and Intuitive Machines (LUNR), each in the low single digits. An equal-weight structure gives smaller suppliers and space names roughly the same influence as the primes.

SHLD is a different animal again. A [July 7 24/7 Wall St. piece](https://247wallst.com/investing/etf/2026/07/07/shld-vs-xar-should-you-play-the-rearmament-boom-with-defense-tech-or-classic-aerospace/) describes it as a global, market-cap-weighted thematic fund with an expense ratio of 0.49% and about $7.5 billion in net assets. Its top three holdings are Lockheed Martin (LMT) at roughly 9.5%, RTX at roughly 7.9% and General Dynamics (GD) at roughly 7.2%, and it also holds Rheinmetall, BAE Systems, Thales, Leonardo, Saab, Hanwha Aerospace and Elbit Systems, alongside Palantir (PLTR).

## What the return gap shows

The Motley Fool comparison put one-year total returns through May 27 at 45.4% for XAR and 32.2% for ITA. The five-year picture was closer: $1,000 grew to about $2,207 in XAR and $2,172 in ITA. The cost of XAR's path was a deeper maximum drawdown, 32.4% versus 21.7% for ITA, and a higher five-year beta, 1.26 versus 1.02.

By July 7, the 24/7 Wall St. comparison showed XAR up 20.18% year to date and 39.14% over the past year, against 0.4% year to date and 10.46% over the past year for SHLD. The same fund that holds the European primes and U.S. defense tech trailed the equal-weight U.S. basket by a wide margin.

Those figures come from different dates, and the sources report some fund statistics inconsistently, so they are best read as a snapshot of relative behavior rather than current standings. The pattern is still clear: the funds most exposed to the largest, most widely owned primes and to European names did not keep pace with the one that spreads weight across mid-cap suppliers and space companies.

## The valuation backdrop

The context is a sector that re-rated sharply before it sold off. [Defense News reported in April](https://www.defensenews.com/news/your-military/2026/04/02/us-defense-stocks-see-no-iran-war-lift-after-early-surge/), citing LSEG data, that the S&P 500 Aerospace & Defense sub-index traded at about 32 times 12-month forward earnings, against roughly 20 times for the broader S&P 500. The NYSE Arca Defense index had fallen nearly 8% in March while the S&P 500 fell about 5%, and European defense stocks lost 11%, their biggest monthly drop since the pandemic.

The same report said analysts attributed the decline mainly to investors unwinding positions after a strong run, with conflict premiums already in prices, rather than to fading demand. It also noted that expected 2026 earnings growth for General Dynamics, Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman (NOC), L3Harris (LHX) and RTX had slipped to about 12% by the end of March from about 15% at the start of the year.

A premium multiple paired with slowing growth expectations is the setup in which fund construction matters most. A cap-weighted fund owns the richest and largest names in proportion to their size. An equal-weight fund owns more of the small and mid-cap companies, which carry more volatility but, in the periods above, also captured more of the upside.

## Budget tailwinds that all three share

All three funds draw on the same demand story. The Defense News report noted a proposed $1.5 trillion U.S. military budget for 2027, against $901 billion approved by Congress for 2026. The 24/7 Wall St. article cites the fiscal 2027 request as including $52.9 billion for critical munitions and $59.7 billion for space, which it says is more than double the fiscal 2026 space level.

Those lines are not evenly distributed across the three baskets. Munitions favor the primes that dominate SHLD and the large-cap end of ITA. Space and autonomy spending is closer to the names that sit near the top of XAR. Request levels are also not appropriations, so what Congress ultimately funds will decide which of these categories actually pay off.

## What to watch

- **Concentration.** ITA's reliance on a handful of holdings, including GE Aerospace, means a commercial aerospace cycle can swamp a defense signal.
- **Fee and drawdown trade-offs.** XAR's lower expense ratio and stronger recent returns came with a deeper drawdown, a reminder that equal weight is a more volatile way to own the sector.
- **Geography.** SHLD's European and Asian holdings tie it to currency moves and to the rearmament schedule overseas, not just to Washington.
- **Overlap in a portfolio.** Holding more than one of these funds can double up on RTX, Lockheed and General Dynamics without adding the diversification the labels suggest.
- **Earnings.** Third-quarter reports will show whether the roughly 12% growth expectation for the large primes holds, which is what the 32 times multiple depends on.
