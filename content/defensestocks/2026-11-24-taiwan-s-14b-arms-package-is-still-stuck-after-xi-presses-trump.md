## What happened

Most defense news this month has been about money Congress might spend. This story is about a sale that has already cleared that hurdle and still sits idle, because one person has not signed it.

Taiwan is seeking a record $14 billion package of weapons and equipment that includes Patriot missiles, an automated command-and-control system and a 360-degree air defense radar, according to [AFP reporting published Nov. 18](https://www.manilatimes.net/2026/11/18/world/asia-oceania/taiwan-awaits-trump-ok-of-14b-arms-sale/2472250). Taiwan has been waiting months for approval. The same report says President Trump stalled the sale after saying in May that it could be used as a bargaining chip with China during trade talks.

[ABC News](https://abcnews.com/International/xi-tells-trump-us-thing-taiwan-avoid-great/story?id=138283818) reports that Congress approved the package in January and that the White House has held it up pending the president's final approval. The [Associated Press](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/trump-weighs-taiwan-arms-package-after-summit-aimed-at-steadying-us-china-ties) likewise reported in May that lawmakers had approved a $14 billion Taiwan arms sale in January and that it cannot move forward until Trump formally sends it to Congress. Trump told reporters then, "I will make a determination."

Sources describe the stage of the process in slightly different words, so treat the exact legal status with care. They agree on the practical point: the package is waiting on Trump.

## The Shenzhen meeting changed little

Trump met Xi Jinping on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen on Nov. 19, their third meeting this year, according to [Focus Taiwan](https://focustaiwan.tw/politics/202611200010). Per a readout from China's state news agency Xinhua, [carried by ABC News](https://abcnews.com/International/xi-tells-trump-us-thing-taiwan-avoid-great/story?id=138283818), Xi said the United States "must keep doing the right thing to avoid the emergence of great turbulence" in relations. ABC's account reported no remarks from Trump, and Focus Taiwan noted that a full account of the meeting had not been made public.

Zack Cooper, a senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute, told Focus Taiwan that "Taiwan probably came up in the meeting, and I don't think the two leaders are likely to have agreed on anything." He did not expect an announcement on the package for at least another month, possibly after the G20 summit, which Xi is expected to attend in Florida in December. He called the package "probably the most important element to watch at the moment."

Taiwan's own message is patient. Deputy Foreign Minister Chen Ming-chi said the timing is "very much up to ... President Trump," that the U.S. midterm results, which AFP says gave Democrats both chambers, would not change it, and that approval "could be just a matter of time." AFP adds that the package could be approved as one piece or in smaller parcels.

## What the stall tells you about demand

The first lesson is that arms demand from a close partner does not convert to orders on its own schedule. A sale can have bipartisan support, be reported as approved by Congress, and still go nowhere while it serves as leverage in a trade and diplomacy track. For anyone modeling revenue tied to Taiwan, the timing variable is a presidential decision, not a budget cycle.

The second is that an earlier Taiwan package shows how large these tranches can be. In December 2025 the administration announced more than $11 billion in sales across eight agreements, according to [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/trump-admin-announces-11b-taiwan-arms-sales-deal.amp). It listed 82 HIMARS launchers and 420 ATACMS missiles worth more than $4 billion combined, 60 self-propelled howitzers worth more than $4 billion, and more than $1 billion each for drones and military software. The AP reported in May that the $11 billion package had also not advanced. Two large packages in the queue means that the political calendar, not production capacity, is setting the pace for Taiwan.

## Which companies this touches

None of the reports above names a manufacturer. That matters: the sources describe Patriot missiles, a command-and-control system and a radar, but they do not say which company would build each item or how many interceptors are involved. Investors should not assume the contractor split from the list alone.

What can be sourced is the supply side of Patriot interceptors. Lockheed Martin (LMT) said on Jan. 6 that a seven-year framework agreement with the Department of War would [raise annual PAC-3 MSE capacity from approximately 600 to 2,000](https://news.lockheedmartin.com/2026-01-06-Lockheed-Martin-and-Department-of-War-Advance-Landmark-Acquisition-Transformation-to-Accelerate-PAC-3-R-MSE-Production), supporting U.S. forces, allies and partner nations. The company said it delivered 620 PAC-3 MSEs in 2025. The Army's July 29 release describes PAC-3 MSE as an [interceptor employed within the Patriot weapon system](https://www.army.mil/article/294215). The reports on Taiwan do not say that its package uses PAC-3 MSE specifically, so the link is context rather than a confirmed order.

The takeaway is narrow. If Taiwan's Patriot missiles are cleared, they would draw on a production base that Lockheed itself says is being expanded for the U.S. and its partners. Capacity is a stated goal, and delivered volume is a figure to check in each quarterly report.

## What to watch next

- **The G20 in Florida in December.** Cooper's read is that a decision is unlikely before then. A meeting between Trump and Xi so soon after Shenzhen could either delay the package further or force a choice.
- **One package or several.** Chen said approval could come in smaller parcels. A split would spread revenue over more notices and make each one harder to attribute to a company.
- **Named contractors.** The formal notifications that accompany a sale are where manufacturers and quantities appear. Until one is sent, the contractor read-through is guesswork.
- **Chip export controls.** Cooper expects tensions between Washington and Beijing to rise over the next couple of months, with further limits on advanced semiconductor exports. That could cut either way for a package Trump has treated as a bargaining chip.
- **Congressional pressure.** Taiwan's deputy foreign minister cites strong bipartisan support in Congress. Whether that turns into a formal push once the new Congress arrives is an open question.

This is information, not investment advice. The only firm facts here are that a $14 billion package exists, that it waits on one decision, and that the leaders' latest meeting produced no public breakthrough. Any company-level conclusion beyond that depends on a notification nobody has seen yet.
