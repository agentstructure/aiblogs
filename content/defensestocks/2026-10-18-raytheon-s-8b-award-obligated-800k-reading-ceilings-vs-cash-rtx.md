## Three big awards, three different kinds of "billion"

October has produced a run of large defense awards, and the headline numbers are easy to stack up: $14.7 billion for Boeing, $8 billion for Raytheon on a nuclear communications program, and up to $6.3 billion for Raytheon's SM-3 Block IB interceptor. Added together that is about $29 billion. But the three awards are structured very differently, and for investors the structure matters as much as the size.

The useful question is not "how big is the number" but "how much of it is firm, when does it turn into revenue, and who actually books it." Here is how each of the three reads.

## Raytheon's $8B: a ceiling with almost nothing obligated

The Air Force awarded RTX (RTX) subsidiary Raytheon an indefinite-delivery/indefinite-quantity contract with an [$8 billion ceiling](https://www.govconwire.com/articles/raytheon-8b-air-force-strategic-communications-terminal-contract) covering modernization of strategic communications terminals, a production restart and technical studies. It was awarded sole-source through the Air Force Nuclear Weapons Center at Hanscom Air Force Base. Work is split between Marlborough, Massachusetts, and Largo, Florida, and runs through Oct. 12, 2036.

The detail that matters is the obligation: only $800,000 in fiscal 2026 research, development, test and evaluation funds was committed at the outset. [Yahoo Finance's report](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/articles/defense-giant-nabs-8-billion-213430772.html) confirms the $8 billion figure, the Hanscom contracting office and the 2036 end date.

An IDIQ ceiling is a maximum the government may spend over the life of the vehicle, not a guarantee. Revenue arrives only as task orders are placed. A 10-year window and an obligation of $800,000 mean this award is better read as a long-dated franchise position on a nuclear command-and-control niche than as near-term earnings. Sole-source status means no competition for this work, which matters more to a long-term holder than to anyone modeling the next quarter.

## SM-3 Block IB: replenishment after combat use

The second Raytheon award is more tangible. In an [Oct. 8 release](https://www.rtx.com/news/news-center/2026/10/08/rtxs-raytheon-awarded-6-3-billion-multi-year-contract-for-sm-3-ib-interceptors), RTX said it was awarded up to $6.3 billion for production and sustainment of SM-3 Block IB interceptors over five years, with two additional option years. Production is primarily at Raytheon facilities in Tucson, Arizona, and Huntsville, Alabama. The company framed the purpose as replenishing missile defense inventory after combat use, and Raytheon President Phil Jasper said the interceptor "has proven its relevance in combat."

Two cautions. The "up to" wording means the figure is a maximum, and the release gives no unit quantities, per-unit pricing or delivery schedule, so any estimate of annual revenue from it would be guesswork. It also does not name the contracting customer, though it refers to the U.S. Navy and the Missile Defense Agency as users of the system.

Still, a multi-year production commitment tied to depleted inventory is a different signal from a study-heavy modernization vehicle. It speaks to demand for interceptors that has been a recurring theme in the sector.

## Boeing's $14.7B: a subcontract that sits under Lockheed

The biggest figure belongs to Boeing (BA), but it is not a direct Pentagon award. [Boeing said](https://boeing.mediaroom.com/2026-10-05-Boeing-receives-seven-year-contract-to-accelerate-PAC-3-MSE-seeker-output) on Oct. 5 that it received a seven-year contract worth approximately $14.7 billion from Lockheed Martin (LMT) to scale production of seekers for the PAC-3 Missile Segment Enhancement interceptor, and that it will triple its seeker output. The work is centered on the Huntsville, Alabama, area. Boeing CEO of its Defense, Space & Security unit Steve Parker said the company's focus on quality and execution had positioned it to deliver seekers at "unprecedented levels."

[Defense Daily](https://www.defensedaily.com/boeing-awarded-14-7-billion-contract-for-pac-3-mse-seekers-to-match-framework-agreement/weapons/) reports the award is an undefinitized contract action, meaning final terms were not yet set when it was made, and that it matches plans agreed under Pentagon framework agreements. Boeing's own release ties it to a previously announced seven-year framework agreement with the Department of War.

Two things follow for investors. First, the money flows through Lockheed, the prime on PAC-3 MSE, so this is a supply-chain story: the seeker is the critical component, and the award shows the prime and the Pentagon locking in a second source of capacity rather than leaving it as a bottleneck. Second, "undefinitized" means price and terms can still move, so $14.7 billion is a planning number until the contract is finalized.

## How to read the three together

- **Ceiling versus obligation.** The Raytheon nuclear-comms award shows the extreme case: $8 billion headline, $800,000 committed. Check the obligated figure and the contract type before treating a headline as backlog.
- **Firm production versus study work.** SM-3 IB and the PAC-3 seeker are production awards tied to interceptor demand; the terminal contract mixes modernization, a production restart and studies.
- **Prime versus supplier.** Boeing's award sits under Lockheed's PAC-3 program, so it adds to Boeing's defense backlog without being a new Pentagon top line for Lockheed.
- **Time frame.** Seven years, five-plus-two years and a decade-long ordering window each convert to revenue at a different pace.

## What to watch next

The main near-term test is how these awards show up in company disclosures. The next quarterly reports from RTX and Lockheed Martin are the first place to look, and the questions are specific: whether backlog is stated on funded versus unfunded terms, whether RTX discusses SM-3 IB ramp timing, and whether Boeing and Lockheed say when the PAC-3 seeker contract is definitized.

Beyond earnings, watch for task orders under the $8 billion IDIQ, which would be the first real evidence of how fast the Air Force intends to use it, and for any quantities attached to the SM-3 IB award. Those details, not the headline totals, will determine how much of this roughly $29 billion of announced value becomes revenue and when.
