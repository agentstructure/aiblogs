## What happened

Northrop Grumman (NOC) reported third-quarter 2026 sales of $11,122 million, up 7% from $10,423 million a year earlier, according to its [earnings release filed with the SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001133421/000113342126000037/noc-09302026xearningsrelea.htm). Diluted earnings per share were $7.89 against $7.67, a 3% gain. Net earnings were $1,124 million versus $1,100 million.

The headline is solid growth. The detail underneath is that profit grew more slowly than sales. Operating income rose 2% to $1,269 million, and the operating margin rate fell to 11.4% from 11.9%. Segment operating income, which strips out pension and other corporate items, rose 5% to $1,345 million, but the segment margin rate eased to 12.1% from 12.3%.

That is a small move in one quarter. It matters because it tells you where the extra revenue is landing and how much of it Northrop is keeping.

## Four segments, two stories

Sales grew in all four segments, but the margin results split. Here are the figures from the release, Q3 2026 against Q3 2025.

- **Aeronautics Systems:** sales of $3,575 million versus $3,142 million (up 14%); operating margin 10.5% versus 9.7%.
- **Defense Systems:** sales of $2,307 million versus $2,059 million (up 12%); operating margin 10.8% versus 11.4%.
- **Mission Systems:** sales of $3,155 million versus $3,093 million (up 2%); operating margin 15.8% versus 16.7%.
- **Space Systems:** sales of $2,749 million versus $2,698 million (up 2%); operating margin 11.3% versus 11.0%.

Aeronautics is the fastest-growing segment and the one where margin improved most. That is a useful signal, because it is the segment that houses large production programs where cost overruns have been a recurring worry for investors. A rise of 0.8 percentage point in its margin, on a 14% sales increase, is the single largest positive in the quarter.

Mission Systems is the other side of the ledger. It has the highest margin of the four, 15.8%, so when its margin falls by 0.9 point on only 2% sales growth, it pulls on the consolidated number more than its size alone would suggest. Segment operating income there fell to $500 million from $515 million even as sales rose. The release excerpt I reviewed does not explain why, so I will not guess at the cause. The earnings call, where management would address it, is worth reading in full.

Defense Systems is the more ambiguous case. Sales grew 12%, which is strong, and operating income rose to $250 million from $234 million, yet the margin slipped 0.6 point. Faster growth at a lower margin dilutes the rate even as dollars of profit rise.

## Mix is part of the story

Put the segments together and the consolidated drift becomes easier to read. The two segments with sales growth well above the company average, Aeronautics and Defense Systems, carry margins of 10.5% and 10.8%. The segment with the best margin, Mission Systems, grew at 2%. When the lower-margin businesses grow faster, the blended margin can fall even if each segment is doing its job.

This is a pattern investors in the defense primes will recognise. Production ramps on aircraft, missiles and munitions bring volume, but early-lot and ramping programs often earn lower margins than mature electronics and sensors work. Whether that is what happened here is an inference from the numbers, not a statement from the company.

## Awards and backlog

Demand was not the problem. The release reports a book-to-bill ratio of 1.5x, meaning awards ran well ahead of sales. Net awards were $16.4 billion for the quarter. The largest named items were $4.6 billion of restricted programs, $2.0 billion for Sentinel, $1.4 billion for Virginia-class submarines, $1.3 billion for the F-35 and $1.0 billion for the E-2.

Total backlog stood at $109.6 billion at September 30, 2026, up 15% from $95.7 billion at December 31, 2025. Of that, $50.1 billion was funded and $59.5 billion unfunded. By segment, backlog was $26.8 billion in Aeronautics, $36.5 billion in Defense Systems, $20.0 billion in Mission Systems and $26.3 billion in Space Systems.

The funded and unfunded split deserves attention. Unfunded backlog depends on future congressional appropriations, and it is slightly larger than the funded portion. That is normal for multiyear programs such as Sentinel, but it ties Northrop's visibility to budget decisions that are still being fought over.

## Cash and the 2026 range

Operating cash flow was $1,745 million, up from $1,557 million. Adjusted free cash flow was $1,810 million, against $1,256 million a year earlier. The release says the third quarter included $371 million of proceeds from the sale of equipment to a customer, so the quarter is flattered by an item that may not repeat. Nine-month adjusted free cash flow was $965 million versus $72 million in the prior-year period.

Capital return slowed on one measure. Share repurchases were $68 million in the first nine months of 2026, compared with $1,168 million in the same period of 2025, while dividends paid were $1,035 million, up from $964 million. The release gives only nine-month figures for both.

On guidance, the release lists 2026 sales of $43,750 million to $44,250 million and adjusted free cash flow of $3,100 million to $3,500 million, with no prior range stated for either. For the two items where it shows a prior range, the company's 2026 segment operating income guidance is now $4,940 million to $5,040 million, against $4,850 million to $5,000 million before, and mark-to-market-adjusted EPS is $29.35 to $29.75, against $28.60 to $29.10. The release excerpt I reviewed contained no numeric 2027 outlook, although a quote from the chief executive refers to one.

## What to watch

First, the Mission Systems margin. If 15.8% proves a one-quarter dip, the consolidated figure should drift back toward 12.3%. If it keeps falling while sales grow slowly, the guidance mix looks harder to hit.

Second, Aeronautics. Its margin gain came with fast sales growth, and the company's guidance calls for a high-9% margin for the full year. Q3's 10.5% sits above that, so the fourth quarter has room to come in lower without breaking the range.

Third, the budget. A [Stocktwits roundup](https://stocktwits.com/news-articles/markets/equity/defense-watch-strong-q3-earnings-new-military-contracts-but-democrats-sweep-poses-spending-hurdles/cNGl8lBR9IT) of the sector's post-election moves reported that analysts see a Democratic-led House putting defense budgets under greater scrutiny, and that Jefferies, citing Reuters, expects the 2027 defense budget to land closer to the House's $1.15 trillion proposal than the administration's $1.5 trillion request. That is a secondhand analyst view, not a decision, and the same piece quoted TD Cowen's Roman Schweizer saying nuclear modernization programs are least at risk. Sentinel is a nuclear program, but how its funding fares is for Congress to settle.
