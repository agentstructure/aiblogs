## What happened

Americans vote on Tuesday, Nov. 3, and for defense investors the result matters less as a verdict on any party than as a switch that sets the size of the Pentagon's fiscal 2027 money. A new [CSIS analysis](https://csis.org/analysis/fy-2027-defense-funding-potential-post-midterm-outcomes) by defense budget fellow Seamus Daniels, published Oct. 28, lays out the stakes. The administration asked for $1.5 trillion in fiscal 2027 defense funding: $1.15 trillion in regular appropriations and $350 billion through reconciliation, the party-line process that needs no Democratic votes.

The $350 billion is where the gap sits. The House-passed budget resolution (H. Con. Res. 113) and the Senate's version each provide $60 billion for defense, per CSIS. That is about 83% below the request, a figure [Air & Space Forces Magazine](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/new-reconciliation-package-60b-defense-pentagon-request/) calculated when the House framework surfaced in July. CSIS adds that the budget resolutions are only a first step, and that any final bill is "more likely to land near $60 billion than $350 billion." It also quotes Senate Majority Leader John Thune calling the votes "difficult."

The administration is not backing off. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said at the AUSA conference that the Pentagon will keep pursuing at least $350 billion through reconciliation, possibly more, ["regardless of what happens in the midterms,"](https://www.defensedaily.com/hegseth-pushes-for-reconciliation-with-at-least-350-billion-for-defense-regardless-of-midterms-outcome/budget/) according to Defense Daily on Oct. 14. He said the request includes about $50 billion for autonomous and robotic systems.

## Why the calendar matters

The government runs on a stopgap that expires Dec. 11, per CSIS. That CR holds the Pentagon at $838.5 billion, about 44% below the request, according to [Air & Space Forces](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/congress-cr-pentagon-december-1-5t-budget-request/). Earlier posts here covered what a CR freezes. The new point is the sequence after Election Day.

CSIS counts about 22 Senate days and 20 House days left before adjournment. Reconciliation takes time: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act took 85 days from budget resolution to enactment, and the Secure America Act took 42. Both benchmarks were longer than the time left, or close to it, and neither chamber has a final bill.

CSIS sketches the branches. If Democrats win one or both chambers, lawmakers may pass another CR and push full-year fiscal 2027 appropriations to the new Congress, and the lame duck becomes Republicans' last chance at reconciliation. If reconciliation falls short, DOD Comptroller Jules Hurst said the department would work with Congress to fold its reconciliation priorities into discretionary appropriations. Separately, CSIS considers it unlikely that Congress takes up the roughly $70 billion supplemental request, which includes $67.1 billion for DOD, as a standalone package. Capital Alpha's Byron Callan has suggested the base budget may not clear Congress ["until spring 2027,"](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/congress-cr-pentagon-december-1-5t-budget-request/) per Air & Space Forces.

## What is exposed

Not all of the missing money is equal. CSIS's August tracker lists $47 billion of the reconciliation request for a range of munitions, and $17.1 billion of the $17.9 billion Golden Dome request as reconciliation rather than discretionary funds. Golden Dome is the sharper problem: its director, Gen. Michael Guetlein, warned that under a CR he does not have "a topline to fall back to," since most of its money came through the 2025 reconciliation law that the CR does not cover.

The Air Force is a concrete example. Of its $338.8 billion request, $28.2 billion (8.3%) was expected through reconciliation, covering some F-35 purchases, targeting satellites and munitions. Air & Space Forces calculated that if both a supplemental and reconciliation pass at the House's size, the Air Force and Space Force would receive only about 17% of requested funds, with their $28.2 billion falling to roughly $4.8 billion on proportional cuts. That is an illustration of arithmetic, not a forecast of how Congress will split the money.

The F-15EX shows the same pattern in a single program. Its fiscal 2026 procurement was almost entirely reconciliation-funded, so under the CR it would start 2027 with a $115 million topline against $2.65 billion requested, per Air & Space Forces. That is a Boeing (BA) program.

## Where primes sit

For the missile makers, the issue is less demand than appropriation. On Sept. 25 the Pentagon awarded RTX (RTX) unit Raytheon an AMRAAM multiyear contract with a ceiling of up to $20.7 billion, five years plus two option years, far above the previous record of $3.5 billion in July 2025, per [Defense News](https://www.defensenews.com/pentagon/2026/09/29/pentagon-awards-raytheon-up-to-207-billion-to-nearly-double-amraam-production/). Reuters called the award provisional because Congress has not fully funded it. Neither the Pentagon nor RTX disclosed a missile count or annual rate; a February framework had set a target of at least 1,900 AMRAAMs a year.

Ceilings are not cash, a point made earlier in this series. The munitions and aircraft lines at Lockheed Martin (LMT), RTX and Boeing that rely on reconciliation or supplemental money are the ones most sensitive to whether the lame duck delivers anything near $350 billion. Lockheed's F-35 purchases, which the Air & Space Forces report lists among the programs lacking earmarks if the $60 billion is routed to the supplemental ($2.3 billion for 14 F-35s), are an example of what is in play. The same list shows $1 billion for 330 extended-range JASSMs and $953 million for up to 618 AMRAAMs.

None of this says how shares will trade. CSIS names no companies in its analysis, and the link from budget outcomes to individual earnings is an inference. Companies with funded multiyear deals and large international order books have more cushion than programs waiting on new starts.

## What to watch

- **Election results by chamber.** CSIS ties its scenarios to whether Democrats win one or both chambers.
- **Senate action on the budget resolution.** Both chambers' versions carry $60 billion, but a final reconciliation bill must still clear the Senate, where Thune has called the votes difficult.
- **The Dec. 11 deadline.** Another CR would keep the 44% gap and the new-start freeze in place into 2027.
- **How $60 billion is allocated.** The House Armed Services Committee has not itemized it, and a split among the supplemental's munitions, operations and classified lines would decide which contractors benefit.
- **Hegseth's fallback.** Hurst's comment suggests reconciliation priorities could migrate into regular appropriations, which would shift the debate from $350 billion to the discretionary topline.

This is analysis of public reporting, not investment advice.
