## What the SEC's scoreboard says

Most crowdfunding headlines come from platforms or data firms. The most neutral scoreboard is the SEC's own, built from EDGAR filings. Its [Regulation Crowdfunding statistics page](https://www.sec.gov/data-research/statistics-data-visualizations/regulation-crowdfunding-cf-offerings), which the SEC lists as last reviewed or updated on Sept. 22, 2026, covers May 16, 2016 through June 30, 2026.

Over that span the page shows 9,851 offerings and $1,644 million in total reported raised. Of those offerings, 4,517 reported proceeds, and the average amount raised per offering reporting proceeds was $364,000. The SEC also counts 1,094 withdrawn offerings.

These are cumulative figures, not a single year's haul. At Dec. 31, 2025, the same page showed 9,461 offerings and $1,546 million reported raised. So the running total moved from $1,546 million to $1,644 million over the first half of 2026. Anyone quoting "Reg CF raised $1.64B" should say it is a decade-plus cumulative number.

## Why $1.64B is a floor, not a final score

The SEC states that the amount-raised totals "likely represent a lower bound estimate." The reason is mechanical. Proceeds come from Form C-U progress updates, and some successful offerings never filed one or left proceeds blank.

The rules explain the gap. Under [17 CFR 227.203](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/227.203), an issuer must file a Form C-U within five business days after it reaches 50 percent and 100 percent of its target. If it accepts money above the target, a final Form C-U is due within five business days after the offering deadline. The progress-update requirement does not apply if the intermediary publicly posts frequent progress updates. The final report still does.

That leaves a visible gap between the 9,851 offerings and the 4,517 that reported proceeds. The SEC's page does not split that gap into causes. It can include offerings that raised nothing, offerings that never filed an update, and offerings that were still open. For a founder, the practical point is that the headline total undercounts money raised and also says little about how often a campaign succeeds.

## Targets are small, ceilings are large, results sit between

The same table shows how differently issuers set their sights. The aggregate target amount, the minimum issuers said they needed, was $662 million, with an average of $67,000. The aggregate maximum offer amount was $9,986 million, with an average of $1,036,000. The ceiling is self-reported by issuers, and the SEC notes it excludes withdrawn offerings. It is a statement of ambition, not a forecast.

Under the [SEC's Regulation Crowdfunding overview](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/exempt-offerings/regulation-crowdfunding), a company can raise a maximum of $5 million through crowdfunding in a 12-month period. Many campaigns set a small floor and a high ceiling so they can keep taking money if demand shows up.

The average hides how uneven results are. In the SEC's May 2025 research paper, [Analysis of Crowdfunding Under the JOBS Act](https://www.sec.gov/files/dera-reg-cf-2505.pdf), which covers filings through Dec. 31, 2024, the average successful offering reported raising about $346,000, while the median was about $113,000. The median target was about $25,000 and the median maximum about $800,000. A handful of large campaigns pull the average well above what a typical issuer raised.

## What kind of company raises this way

The same paper profiles the issuer. The median issuer had around $80,000 in total assets, including $13,000 in cash, $60,000 in debt, $10,000 in revenue and three employees. The SEC staff found that only about one in seven issuers had recorded a net profit at the time of the offering.

In other words, Reg CF mostly funds very small, very young companies, many of them raising capital for the first time. That is the case for the exemption, since these firms often cannot reach institutional investors. It is also the risk, since investors are backing businesses with thin balance sheets and short operating records.

## The exit question

For retail investors, the harder question is what happens next. The DERA paper tracked issuers that reported proceeds on Form C-U between 2016 and 2024. It covered 3,253 unique issuers. The staff identified 8 IPOs (0.25%), 71 acquisitions (2.2%) and 110 issuers that later received venture capital financing (3.4%).

Two cautions apply. The acquisition and venture capital counts were found by matching issuers against a Refinitiv (LSEG) database, so they may miss deals that were never recorded there. And the staff identified a few additional listings among issuers that raised money but did not report proceeds, which the tabulation excludes. Even so, the pattern is plain: the typical Reg CF investment is an illiquid stake in a company whose likeliest outcomes are staying private, being sold for a small price or closing.

## What it costs to raise this way

The DERA paper also estimated intermediary pay. Total compensation, including cash and securities commissions and listing fees, came to about 7.7% to 8.1%. The average intermediary commission was about 6.5% of proceeds. In around a third of offerings, and close to half of those with proceeds, the intermediary also took a securities stake in addition to the cash commission. Where disclosed, that stake averaged about 2.5%.

Founders should treat that as a baseline when comparing platforms, and read the Form C closely for fees paid in equity, because those dilute existing holders.

## What to do with this data

For issuers, the numbers argue for realism. Set a target you can reach, since the median raise is far below the average. Budget for fees of roughly 8 percent. Plan your Form C-U filings, because they make your result visible to the SEC and to anyone tracking the market.

For investors and analysts, the lesson is to read crowdfunding totals with their source and period attached. A platform's monthly figure, a data firm's quarterly count and the SEC's cumulative total measure different things. The SEC itself warns that its statistics come from EDGAR filings, may contain errors and can change as data are revised.

Watch the next SEC snapshot, which would cover the period through December 2026, and compare how much the proceeds total moves against the offering count. If proceeds reporting improves, the floor rises without more money being raised, so read year-over-year changes with that in mind.
