## A headline that hides the story

StartEngine, a retail investing platform that hosts Reg CF and Reg A raises, reported third-quarter revenue of $38.6 million, up 71% from $22.6 million a year earlier, according to its [Form 10-Q for the period ended September 30, 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1661779/000110465926120097/stgc-20260930x10q.htm). Read only that line and it looks like a banner quarter for equity crowdfunding.

The filing's revenue table says otherwise. The lines that serve startups and small issuers all shrank. The growth came from a different business: StartEngine Private, which sells stakes in late-stage private companies, and from Vinovest, a wine and whiskey investing business the company acquired in March 2026.

For founders planning a Reg CF or Reg A raise, this is a useful data point. It comes from a platform's own accounts, not a marketing deck, and it shows where that platform's business is moving.

## What fell, by how much

Here is how the issuer-facing lines moved in the third quarter, all from the 10-Q's revenue table:

- **Regulation Crowdfunding platform fees:** $1,342,902, down from $2,104,571, a drop of $761,669 or about 36%.
- **Regulation A commissions:** $362,002, down from $653,056, about 45% lower.
- **StartEngine Premium** (paid services for issuers): $532,170, down from $730,500, about 27% lower.

Added together, those three lines brought in about $2.24 million in the quarter versus about $3.49 million a year earlier, a decline of roughly 36% (our arithmetic from the filing's figures). Reg CF fees were about 3.5% of total quarterly revenue, compared with about 9.3% a year earlier.

The nine-month picture is the same, if slightly less severe. Reg CF platform fees were $4.73 million against $6.27 million, down about 25%. Reg A commissions fell from $2.90 million to $1.29 million, about 56%. Premium fell from $2.32 million to $1.23 million, about 47%.

## What the company says caused it

The company's explanation is specific. On Reg CF, the filing says the decrease "was primarily driven by lower raise totals for issuers," that StartEngine "hosted fewer raises of successful issuers that had decreased demand from issuers compared to 2025," and that it had fewer live raises in the quarter. Put plainly, there were fewer campaigns on the platform, and the ones that ran raised less.

On Reg A, the company attributes the drop to lower amounts raised by its issuers and notes that the line is volatile because so few Reg A offerings are hosted. On Premium, it cites a shift in payment strategy and fewer issuers using the services.

One more detail is worth noting. The filing says StartEngine's own Regulation A offering, which began in July 2026, was later terminated, and that advertising costs for it were expensed. The company does not give a reason for the termination in the sections we reviewed, so we don't speculate on one here.

## Where the growth came from

StartEngine Private generated $31.16 million of the quarter's $38.64 million in revenue, about 81%, from the closing of 12 offerings. Add Vinovest's $3.45 million and the two account for roughly 90% of the total.

Over nine months, Private revenue rose $11.6 million to $87.5 million, on 59 closings versus 55 in the same period of 2025.

That growth was expensive to produce. The filing says the gross margin on Private offerings fell to about 26% in the quarter from about 32%, which it blames on "increased competition for investments in late-stage private companies and the sale of offerings in larger increments, which required lower pricing to remain competitive." Company-wide gross profit was $9.79 million on $38.64 million of revenue, a gross margin of about 25%, against about 37% a year earlier (our calculation).

Operating expenses rose 81% to $13.06 million, driven mainly by headcount supporting Private offerings and sales. The result was a net loss of about $3.14 million, versus net income of about $1.16 million in Q3 2025. For the nine months, the company reported a net loss of about $3.54 million versus net income of about $6.16 million a year earlier.

## What founders should take from it

First, the filing is evidence about platform economics, not about any individual campaign. It tells us StartEngine hosted fewer successful raises and that issuers' demand softened. It does not tell us whether that reflects the whole Reg CF market, and a single company's 10-Q should not be read as an industry statistic. Other platforms may look different.

Second, platform incentives matter. A portal whose fastest-growing line is a lower-margin product for late-stage private stakes has reasons to put its marketing and sales headcount there. The filing says the increase in operating costs was driven by additional headcount supporting StartEngine Private. Founders choosing a portal should ask how much promotion, investor email and sales attention a campaign will actually get, and compare that across platforms rather than assuming the largest brand delivers the most.

Third, fee income depends on raise size. When a platform's Reg CF revenue falls 36% in a quarter while it describes fewer live raises and lower totals, a campaign with a modest target and a ready-made audience of customers or followers is a safer plan than one that counts on the platform's own investor base to fill the round.

## What to watch next

Three things are worth tracking, none of them settled by this filing. The first is whether Reg CF fees stabilize in the fourth quarter, which will show whether Q3 was a trough or the start of a trend. The second is whether StartEngine's Private margins keep compressing, since the filing says the company expects to keep exploring ways to keep the offerings competitive, which may continue to affect margins. The third is the next quarterly filing, which will show whether the issuer demand the company describes as weaker has recovered.

Revenue growth at a crowdfunding platform and activity for crowdfunding issuers are not the same thing. This quarter, they moved in opposite directions.
