## Two fund closes, one market

Two venture firms announced fund closes in the past week, and they sit at opposite ends of today's market. Norwest [closed Norwest 18 at $4 billion](https://www.uniindia.com/business-wire-india/norwest-announces-4-billion-global-multi-stage-fund-102682/243472), the largest fund in its 65-year history, lifting capital under management to $18 billion. Vine Ventures [closed its third fund at $236 million](https://hedgeco.net/news/11/2026/vine-ventures-closed-an-oversubscribed-236-million-third-fund-for-the-us-israel-technology-corridor.html), above its initial target, which took the firm past $500 million in assets under management five years after its founding.

Both are good outcomes. But the data behind them says something less comfortable about who gets funded, which matters to any manager planning a raise.

The clearest picture comes from the [Q3 2026 PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor](https://nvca.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Q3-2026-PitchBook-NVCA-Venture-Monitor.pdf). Through the end of September, US venture funds raised $108.5 billion across 699 funds, up 38.6% from the $78.3 billion raised in all of 2025. On the headline number, fundraising has recovered. The distribution underneath it has not.

## The numbers behind the headline

PitchBook reports that megafunds of $500 million or larger account for 78.1% of capital raised this year, while making up just 6% of new funds closed. Established managers, defined as those on their fourth fund or later, took 88.2% of all capital raised. PitchBook describes that as the highest share in its dataset and notes the historical range was roughly 50% to 80%, so 2026 is the first year to break decisively above it.

A single firm illustrates the pull of the top. Andreessen Horowitz closed funds totaling $23.8 billion through Q3, per the same report, which is more than 20% of all US venture capital raised this year. That includes its $8.5 billion Growth Fund V. Bessemer Venture Partners announced two funds in Q3 totaling $5.8 billion, a $1.8 billion early-stage fund and a $4 billion growth-stage fund.

The other end looks very different:

- Just $4.9 billion went into 81 first-time funds year to date, with the annualized count on pace for the fewest in a decade.
- The number of distinct emerging managers closing at least one fund fell from 927 in 2022 to 440 in 2025 to 211 through Q3 2026.
- Average fund size rose 87.2% year over year to $165.4 million, while the median settled at $10 million after a fifth consecutive annual decline.

The gap between a $165.4 million average and a $10 million median is the signature of a few very large funds and many very small ones.

## Where the middle went

The most useful finding for managers is what happened to the band between the extremes. PitchBook says funds between $100 million and $500 million made up roughly 17% of all funds closed in the years leading up to 2021. Through Q3 2026, that share has fallen to 10%, the lowest in its dataset.

PitchBook calls this the mechanical result of managers either scaling up into the multistage, megafund tier or staying small and specialized at pre-seed and seed. It also names the risk for a midsize manager: being squeezed from both sides, too large to compete on speed and flexibility with the smallest seed funds, and too small to write the check sizes AI-native companies now command.

Vine's $236 million fund falls squarely inside that band, which makes its result notable. By HedgeCo's account, the raise came above target and was supported by early seed positions in AI and infrastructure companies. Per Calcalist, as cited in the same write-up, Vine's earlier funds were $50 million and $140 million, so Fund III is a clear step up in size. The article also says LP commitments remain selective and that capital is concentrating in the largest platforms. Vine's raise is evidence that a midsize fund can still close above target. It is one data point, and PitchBook's counts say it is the harder path.

## Why LPs are behaving this way

PitchBook ties the concentration to liquidity. Limited partners have been short of distributions for a couple of years, and the report says LPs that receive cash from a manager with a proven record tend to recommit it to that manager or move it elsewhere to hold allocation targets steady, rather than redirect it to smaller or newer funds. The report adds that it does not expect that preference to shift structurally until LPs see real relief on the distribution side.

It also describes a flywheel: multistage firms draw outsized commitments, raise bigger funds, and are positioned to write the largest checks into the fastest-growing AI companies. Those companies' early wins, the report says, reinforce LP conviction in the same funds. It names accelerating IPOs from leading AI companies as possible, but uneven, relief.

Norwest's release shows the same logic from the manager's side. Its announcement says that since Norwest 17 launched in April 2024, the firm has made more than 87 investments and that over 30 portfolio companies have reached liquidity events, including IPOs of Omada Health, Swiggy and BillionToOne and acquisitions by Salesforce and ServiceNow. Norwest 17 was a $3 billion fund, so Norwest 18 is a third larger. A visible distribution record is the argument an LP wants to hear right now.

The private equity side shows a similar pull toward demonstrated demand. Gemspring Capital [closed its fourth buyout fund at a $3.5 billion hard cap](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/gemspring-capital-closes-fourth-buyout-fund-at-3-5-billion-hard-cap-302939881.html) on Nov. 16, in less than three months and in a single close, according to its release. Gemspring did not disclose the size of its prior buyout funds, so there is no step-up figure to compare.

## What managers and founders can take from it

For fund managers, the practical reading is about positioning. A manager in the $100 million to $500 million range needs a clear reason to exist: a defined stage or sector edge, a track record that shows realized returns, or access to deals the megafunds cannot reach. 

For emerging managers, with only 81 first-time funds closed through Q3, the numbers argue for modeling a longer raise.

For founders, the implication runs in two directions. Megafund-backed rounds will keep getting larger, since those funds hold the capital. But the thinning of the middle means fewer midsize lead investors for Series A and B rounds that do not need AI-scale checks. Founders building a target list should ask each firm when its fund closed and how much dry powder remains, rather than assuming a recent headline means capacity.

## What to watch next

Three things will show whether the pattern holds. First, Q4 fundraising figures, which will show whether the 78.1% megafund share rises or eases as more managers close year-end funds. Second, whether the emerging manager count of 211 keeps falling or stabilizes. Third, distributions: PitchBook ties any broadening of LP appetite to real liquidity, so the pace of IPOs and acquisitions of venture-backed companies is the variable that decides whether the middle recovers.

This is information and commentary, not investment advice. PitchBook-NVCA figures cover US venture funds. Norwest and Vine invest partly outside the US, so they illustrate the size bands rather than count toward those totals.
