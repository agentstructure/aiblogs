## The headline number hides the shape

US startups raised $20.83 billion across 563 rounds in October, according to [AlleyWatch's monthly report](https://alleywatch.com/2026/11/us-venture-capital-statistics-october-2026). That is up 71% from October 2025 and down 25% from September. On its face, it is a strong month.

The distribution tells a different story. Forty-three rounds of $100 million or more totaled $14.25 billion, or 68% of the month's dollars. The other 520 rounds shared roughly $6.6 billion, a figure derived by subtracting the large rounds from the monthly total. The average round was $37.0 million, but an average pulled up by a handful of giants says little about what a typical founder will see.

Four rounds of $1 billion or more accounted for $5.35 billion on their own, about 26% of the month:

- Ramp: $1.85 billion, Series G
- Tenstorrent: $1.5 billion, Series E
- Flapping Airplanes: $1 billion, Series A
- Kalshi: $1 billion, Series G

AlleyWatch counts only rounds with disclosed amounts, so real totals may be higher.

## What the biggest checks looked like

The four largest rounds had little in common except size, and that is the useful lesson.

Ramp told investors on October 15 that it had closed a $1.85 billion primary financing at a $60 billion valuation, led by Dragoneer Investment Group, [according to the law firm that advised Dragoneer](https://www.gunder.com/en/news-insights/client-news/dragoneer-leads-ramps-dollar185-billion-financing-at-dollar60-billion-valuation). The firm's announcement says Ramp reports more than $2 billion in annualized revenue. A company with that revenue base is raising on a growth-equity script: proven scale and a valuation set against real revenue.

Tenstorrent announced a $1.5 billion Series E at a $6.2 billion post-money valuation on October 19, [as summarized by Angel Investors Network](https://angelinvestorsnetwork.com/angel-investing/ai-funding-rounds-this-week-tenstorrent-1-5b-2026), citing the company and TechCrunch. The stated use of funds is volume manufacturing and global commercial expansion, which is capital for building physical capacity.

Flapping Airplanes is the outlier. The same summary, citing Crunchbase News, describes a $1 billion Series A led by Lux Capital and Index Ventures, arriving less than 10 months after a $180 million seed round. It is an exception, not a template. Founders pitching a pre-revenue company should not read it as a sign that Series A investors are writing checks of that size across the board.

The practical takeaway is that each megaround is a different kind of bet, and each is funded by investors who are comfortable with concentration. If your business does not look like one of them, benchmarking your raise against the top of the table will mislead you.

## Where ordinary rounds sit

The rest of the table is much quieter. AlleyWatch reports 341 early-stage rounds, a bucket that covers pre-seed, seed, angel, accelerator and incubator deals, totaling $1.98 billion. That is about 61% of all rounds by count and about 9.5% of the dollars. Dividing the two gives an average near $5.8 million, and AlleyWatch gives a median of $2.7 million for the group. The largest early-stage deal was Dream Labs' $90 million seed.

Series A shows a similar split. There were 121 Series A rounds worth $5.11 billion, up from $2.97 billion in September. Flapping Airplanes alone was $1 billion of that. Strip it out and the other 120 rounds averaged roughly $34 million, a figure computed from AlleyWatch's totals. Series B had 50 rounds totaling $2.98 billion, and late-stage (Series C and later) had 51 rounds totaling $10.77 billion, just over half of all capital.

Two things stand out for a founder planning a raise:

1. **Mid-stage money is still there.** Series A and B together drew about $8.1 billion across 171 rounds. That is a real market, even if it gets less attention than the billion-dollar headlines.
2. **The median early round is small.** At $2.7 million, a typical seed raise is a fraction of what the largest seeds command, so set your target against peers at your stage rather than against the biggest announcement of the month.

AI dominated the dollars. AlleyWatch tags about $13.76 billion, roughly two-thirds of the month, to AI companies. Fintech, led by Ramp and Kalshi, raised $2.85 billion combined. New York led the states AlleyWatch covers with $4.94 billion, or 23.7% of US capital, across 54 rounds.

## The supply side: many small funds

Capital concentration at the top sits alongside a crowded field of new managers. A [ZInvestorHunting tally of SEC Form D filings](https://zinvestorhunting.com/guides/new-venture-funds-last-quarter) counts 869 new venture funds from 642 firms in the third quarter of 2026, up 11% from 783 in the second quarter and up 67% from 519 a year earlier. The page notes that late filings may still raise the count.

These funds skew small. The median stated target was $20 million, 72.2% of funds with a stated target were under $50 million, and only 33 funds, or 5.4%, targeted $250 million or more. The combined target across the 609 funds that stated one was $38.7 billion, with ARCH Venture Fund XIV at $3 billion the largest.

For founders, that mix matters. Most new vehicles are small, which suggests a large pool of investors who write checks in the early-stage range where the median round sits. Fund size shapes check size, ownership targets and how many follow-on rounds an investor can support, so ask about it in the first meeting.

## What to do with this

This is information, not advice, but a few reads follow from the data.

First, treat monthly totals with caution. A single month's dollars swing with when the largest deals close. October fell 25% from September even as it ran 71% above a year earlier, and two or three giant rounds can move either comparison.

Second, build your comparables from the median, not the mean. If your plan assumes a round near the average of $37 million, check how many rounds of that size actually closed. With 43 rounds at $100 million or more and 341 early-stage rounds averaging a few million dollars, the middle is thin in this snapshot.

Third, expect investors to be selective in the middle while capital pools at the top. A founder with traction in AI or fintech has the strongest backdrop in the data. A founder outside those categories should lean on fundamentals investors can underwrite: revenue growth, margins and a believable use of proceeds.

## What to watch next

AlleyWatch's November report will show whether October's pace holds or whether the megarounds were a one-month cluster. Watch the Series A count and total excluding the largest deal, since that is the cleaner read on the mid-stage market. Also watch whether the Q4 Form D count of new funds keeps climbing, which would add more small-check investors to the field.

One caveat on the numbers: they come from a single tracker's monthly tally, with the largest rounds cross-checked against company and press announcements. Trackers differ on methodology and revise totals as data arrives, so use these figures to understand the shape of the market and not as a precise measure.
