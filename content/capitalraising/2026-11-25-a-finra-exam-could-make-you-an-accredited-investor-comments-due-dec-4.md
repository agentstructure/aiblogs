## What the SEC is actually asking

If you raise money under Regulation D, the most important definition in your offering is "accredited investor." It decides who can buy in a Rule 506(c) round, how many non-accredited buyers a Rule 506(b) round can carry, and how much paperwork you owe if you take any.

For years, an individual qualified in two main ways: money or a job. Money means a net worth above $1 million excluding a primary residence, or income above $200,000 (or $300,000 jointly with a spouse or spousal equivalent) in each of the last two years. The SEC's own [exam notice](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/33-11445.pdf) recites those tests. A third route, added in 2020, lets the SEC designate professional credentials by order.

Now the Commission is using that third route. The notices were [published in the Federal Register on October 5, 2026](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/33-11445.pdf), and comments are due on or before December 4, 2026. They are notices requesting comment, not proposed rule amendments. Any designation would come later, by SEC order. That distinction matters for planning: nothing about your current offering documents changes today.

Two of the notices are worth reading in full. One asks whether to designate [passing an accredited investor exam to be developed by FINRA](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/33-11445.pdf) (File No. 4-931). The other asks whether to designate [holding a U.S. CPA license in good standing](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/33-11446.pdf) (File No. 4-932). Morrison Foerster's [summary](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/261001-sec-seeks-comment-new-pathways) says the package also covers other credentials, including the CFA charter and CFP certification. Both that summary and Proskauer's [separate one](https://www.proskauer.com/alert/sec-advances-retailization-agenda-through-a-potential-accredited-investor-exam-and-registered-fund-reforms) are secondhand, so check the primary notices before relying on any list of credentials.

## The exam: open to anyone 18 or older

The exam is the novel piece. According to the SEC notice, which says it relies on discussions with FINRA staff, the test would be open to anyone age 18 or older and would not require association with a FINRA member firm. It would follow the format of FINRA's Securities Industry Essentials exam: roughly 75 multiple-choice questions and about two hours.

The content ranges across how securities are structured, investment risk, disclosure requirements, financial statements, conflicts of interest and corporate governance. The notice gives target weightings. Investment risks carry the most weight, at 20% to 28% of the questions, while conflicts of interest and corporate governance each account for 10% to 18%.

Passing would be valid for ten years. After that, a holder would have to retake and pass the exam to keep relying on it. The notice says FINRA does not anticipate granting waivers. A candidate who fails could retake after 30 calendar days, and someone who fails three or more times in succession within two years would wait 180 days. The fee is paid on each attempt.

Two guardrails are worth noting. Status would be personal: exam holders [could not use it to buy securities on behalf of another person](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/33-11445.pdf). And FINRA would build a process so issuers and others can independently verify that someone passed, along with the passage date and the date the ten-year window ends.

## Why issuers should care: verification

The practical value for a founder or fund manager may not be a flood of new investors. It may be easier paperwork.

In a Rule 506(c) offering, you may advertise publicly, but you must take reasonable steps to verify that every purchaser is accredited. Verifying income or net worth means collecting tax forms, bank statements or a letter from a professional. The SEC notice argues that confirming a credential that can be looked up publicly [may be less costly than verifying financial eligibility](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/33-11445.pdf) under the other prongs. The CPA notice makes a similar point: the public can check a license through NASBA's CPAverify.org or a state board website.

There is a second benefit in Rule 506(b). If every purchaser is accredited, you avoid the disclosure the rule requires when non-accredited investors participate. The SEC notice acknowledges that an issuer with only accredited buyers may face lower costs. It applies the same logic to Tier 2 Regulation A and Regulation Crowdfunding offerings, where investment limits depend on accredited status.

Be careful with the upside, though. The SEC says in the notice that it is [difficult to quantify how many additional people would qualify](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/33-11445.pdf), because it cannot predict how many will take and pass the exam or how many already qualify on net worth or income. It also notes that some people who are already accredited may take the exam simply to make verification easier, which would not expand the pool at all. The Commission adds that issuers who would have raised from existing accredited investors anyway may see limited extra capital.

For scale, the notice cites an SEC staff report putting Regulation D offerings, excluding pooled funds, at approximately $400 billion between July 1, 2024 and June 30, 2025. That is the market in which any new investor class would operate.

## What to do before December 4

None of this is advice on a particular offering. But if you raise capital, a few questions are worth working through with counsel.

First, decide whether you have a view. Comment letters go to the SEC through the online form or by email, referencing File Nos. 4-931 and 4-932, per the [notice](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/33-11445.pdf). A founder who has spent weeks chasing verification letters has useful evidence about what the current system costs.

Second, look at your investor base. If your angels or fund investors hold a CPA license, the CPA notice describes a verification route that is free and public, if the SEC adopts it. Holders would need an active license: the notice says [inactive or suspended licenses would not be in good standing](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/33-11446.pdf).

Third, watch the sequencing. Even if the Commission moves quickly after the comment window closes, the exam still has to be built by FINRA and then designated by order, according to [Proskauer's analysis](https://www.proskauer.com/alert/sec-advances-retailization-agenda-through-a-potential-accredited-investor-exam-and-registered-fund-reforms). Do not tell prospective investors that an exam route exists yet. It does not.

Finally, keep your own verification files. Whatever the SEC adopts, the issuer still carries the obligation to form a reasonable belief that each purchaser qualifies. A public database makes that easier to document, but it does not transfer the responsibility.

## What to watch next

Three things will tell you how far this goes. First, how many comments argue that a 75-question test is too light a screen for private-market risk, and how the Commission responds. Second, whether the SEC designates the CPA license and other credentials first, since those need no new exam. Third, how FINRA describes the verification process, because an issuer-facing lookup is what would make the exam useful in a 506(c) round.

The comment window closes nine days from today, and Thanksgiving falls in between. If your company relies on Regulation D, read the notices rather than a summary of them.
