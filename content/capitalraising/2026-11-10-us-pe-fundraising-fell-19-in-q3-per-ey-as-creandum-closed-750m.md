## Two fundraising signals, one week apart

Creandum, the European early-stage firm, closed Fund VIII at about $750 million on November 2, according to [Tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2026/11/02/lovable-and-black-forest-labs-backer-creandum-closes-750m-fund-had/) and [Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/news/168819-creandum-closes-750m-fund-its-largest-to-back-early-stage-startups/). It is the firm's largest fund to date. The previous vehicle, Fund VII, was EUR 500 million, or about $560 million, per Tech.eu.

Just days earlier, EY published its third-quarter read on US private equity. [EY's US PE Pulse](https://www.ey.com/en_us/private-equity/us-private-equity-industry-insights) says capital raised fell 19% quarter on quarter to $93.3 billion in 3Q26, while the fund count fell 33% to 122. Year to date through the third quarter, US private equity managers raised $283.4 billion across 447 funds, against $312.7 billion across 496 funds in the same period of 2025.

Those two facts sit side by side without contradicting each other. One experienced venture manager raised a bigger fund. The broader US private equity pool shrank, and it shrank more in the number of funds than in dollars. Fewer funds are getting through, and the ones that do are not small.

This post is a read-across, not a claim that venture and private equity share a cause. They are different asset classes with different limited partner bases. But the pattern in the numbers is the one founders and fund managers should plan around.

## What Creandum's close actually shows

Start with the arithmetic. $750 million against roughly $560 million for Fund VII is an increase of about a third, by our calculation from the Tech.eu figures. That is a step-up, not a leap. Managers who raise a fund one-third larger than its predecessor are making a credible case to the same pool of investors, not asking them to reprice the firm.

The terms of deployment are also conventional. Per Tech.eu, Creandum plans to put half the fund into new investments and half into follow-on rounds, focused on seed and Series A. That reserve ratio matters. A fund that holds half its capital back for follow-ons is telling its LPs it expects its best companies to need multiple rounds and wants to keep its ownership as they grow.

The firm did not name its investors. Tech.eu quotes it describing them only as "multiple elite US and European institutional investors." Treat any claim about who anchored the fund as unverified until Creandum or a regulatory filing says otherwise.

Creandum also used the announcement to point at geography. It says roughly half of European tech capital over the past year came from US investors, while very little US tech funding came from Europe, and that US firms increasingly compete for European deals, per Tech.eu. In response it opened a new San Francisco office, building on a presence there since 2012, with US staff working alongside teams in Stockholm, London and Berlin.

For European founders, that is a signal about who is pricing their rounds. For managers, it is a reminder that a fund's home market no longer limits its competition.

## What the EY numbers add

EY's page makes the private equity side concrete. Capital raised fell 19% in the quarter. The count of funds closing fell by a third. Distributions, the cash that returns to LPs and funds new commitments, remain the constraint: EY says they were nearly 15% of net asset value in 2025, below the 2013-22 average of about 24%.

When LPs get back less cash than they expect, they have less to recommit. That is the mechanism behind many of the slower closes managers describe, and EY's figures fit it. EY's GP survey, drawn from about 50 private equity respondents, found LP pressure on distributions averaging 6.1 on a 10-point scale, with 34% of GPs rating it 8 or higher, according to [EY's Q3 pulse](https://www.ey.com/en_us/insights/private-equity/pulse). Because the sample is small, read that as sentiment, not measurement.

There are some signs of relief in the same report. EY counted 126 deals valued at $228 billion announced in Q3, up 43% by value from Q2, and $212 billion in announced exits, up 15% from Q2 and 17% from a year earlier. In the survey, 42% of GPs expect distributions to rise this year, 48% expect them to stay flat and 10% expect a decline.

Dry powder is the figure to be careful with. EY puts US private equity dry powder at about $1.15 trillion as of 1Q26 and says third-quarter data will not be available until January 2027. Other sites quote different totals, including global figures, that do not reconcile with each other. This post uses only EY's number, with its date.

## What this means if you are raising

**Track record sets the size of the step-up.** Creandum's increase is moderate and its strategy is unchanged. In a market where fewer funds close, a first-time or second-time manager should not assume a one-third step-up is the norm for them. Build the target from your portfolio's realized and marked results.

**Expect LPs to ask about liquidity.** With distributions below their long-run average, LPs weigh cash back before they weigh new vintages. A manager who can show exits, secondary sales or distributions is answering the question investors are asking.

**Show a reserve plan.** Creandum's half-and-half split is a clear, checkable commitment. A vague reserve policy is harder to underwrite.

**Name the competition.** If US capital is bidding for European deals, say how you win them. The same goes in reverse for any regional manager.

**Founders: ask how big the fund is.** A seed-and-Series-A fund with half its capital in reserve can lead your next round or follow into it. Ask which, and what the plan is if the next round is flat or down.

## What to watch next

Three things will show whether the split continues. First, EY's January 2027 update, which should add third-quarter dry powder and let readers compare it with the 1Q26 figure. Second, whether the exit total EY counted converts into cash back to LPs, since announced exits are not completed distributions. Third, whether other early-stage managers close at similar step-ups or whether Creandum turns out to be the exception.

Until then, the data points one way: capital is available, but it is going to a narrower set of managers, in larger checks, with more questions about cash returned. That is information and commentary, not advice on any specific fund or investment.
