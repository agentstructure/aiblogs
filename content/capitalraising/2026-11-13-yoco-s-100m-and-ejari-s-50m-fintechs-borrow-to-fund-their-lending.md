## Two facilities, one pattern

Two private credit facilities announced in the past week share a feature that matters for any founder weighing debt: neither one is meant to pay salaries or extend runway. Each funds a book of loans or receivables that the borrower originates.

The larger deal is Yoco's. The Cape Town payments company [secured a $100 million (R1.6 billion) credit facility](https://businesstech.co.za/news/?p=883352) from Victory Park Capital Advisors, a Chicago-based firm that manages about $3.6 billion in assets, according to a report published on 9 November. [Innovation Village](https://innovation-village.com/south-african-fintech-yoco-secures-100-million-credit-facility-from-victory-park-capital-advisors/) carried the same amount and lender. The reports describe it as Yoco's first move into the debt market.

The second is Ejari's. The Saudi proptech [expanded its Shariah-compliant Murabaha warehouse facility](https://thestartupscene.me/INVESTMENTS/Saudi-Proptech-Ejari-Expands-Rental-Financing-Facility-to-50-Million) with Partners for Growth (PFG), a California-based private credit provider, to $50 million (SAR 187.5 million). The two companies have worked together since 2024, and Ejari did not disclose the prior facility size, the amount drawn or the repayment terms.

Together the two facilities total $150 million. Techloy's weekly roundup says that [was more than half of the $258.6 million](https://www.techloy.com/week-46-africa-middle-east-startup-funding-yoco/) in disclosed funding across Africa and the Middle East that week. That figure comes from a single roundup, so treat it as a rough indicator of how much of the region's capital arrived as debt rather than equity.

## What each facility actually funds

Yoco's money has a specific job. BusinessTech reports the facility will finance cash advances to merchants, which range from R2,000 to R1.5 million and can be used for staff, stock, equipment or new branches. Yoco said the facility gives it "direct control over the financing needs for our merchants at scale and in-house." Until now, BusinessTech notes, Yoco had disbursed R6.5 billion to merchants through partner lenders. The new debt lets it fund those advances itself.

Ejari's structure is narrower still. Its facility finances the rental transactions behind its Rent Now, Pay Later product: landlords receive a year's rent up front, and tenants repay in monthly instalments. The Startup Scene reports the financing is backed by the underlying rental contracts rather than allocated to general operations. Ejari also says it has received requests to facilitate more than $500 million in rental payments, but the article is careful to note that this measures demand, not completed transactions or deployed financing.

In both cases the collateral is the borrower's own receivables. That is the core difference from the venture debt most startup founders know.

## How this differs from venture debt

A conventional venture loan is underwritten against the company: its investors, its cash burn, its path to the next round or exit. Lenders typically ask for warrants or covenants because the borrower might fail before repaying. A warehouse or receivables facility is underwritten against the assets in the pool. The lender looks at how those loans or contracts perform, how quickly they repay and how losses behave, and advances against them.

That changes what a founder needs to bring. Equity investors can be won with a growth story. An asset-backed lender wants repayment data: vintage curves, delinquency, collection mechanics. Yoco's model is a good illustration. Repayment is tied to merchants' card sales, which flow through Yoco's own payments system. That gives a lender visibility into the cash that services the loan, though the reports do not detail Victory Park's terms, pricing or advance rates, and neither does this article.

None of this makes the debt cheap or risk-free. Pricing, maturity and covenants were not disclosed for either deal, and warehouse lines typically sit alongside conditions that can restrict a borrower if portfolio performance slips. Founders reading headline dollar amounts should remember that a facility is a ceiling on borrowing, not cash on the balance sheet. Ejari's undisclosed drawn amount is a reminder of the gap.

## The wider debt market

The deals arrive in a venture debt market that has grown quickly. Capital Advisors Group's [Q3 2026 update](https://www.capitaladvisors.com/research/debt-market-update-q3-2026/), published on 3 November, puts year-to-date venture debt volume at $81.2 billion, with full-year 2026 on track to exceed $81.7 billion against $69.7 billion for 2025. Venture growth credit made up about 80 percent of dollar volume ($65.0 billion) but only 48 percent of deals (240 of 498). The median deal size was $14.9 million, more than three times the 2025 median of $4.25 million.

Capital Advisors also notes that Q2 headline volume of about $45 billion included a single $20 billion senior notes facility for SpaceX. Strip it out and underlying Q2 volume was roughly $24 billion to $25 billion. Capital Advisors counts Q3 volume at $36.7 billion across 345 deals. Note that a few very large transactions can swing these totals, and different trackers use different definitions of venture debt, so the figures are best read as a direction of travel rather than a precise measure.

The data points the same way as the two deals: dollars concentrate in larger, later-stage facilities, and much of the money is structured against assets rather than against the company's equity story. A seed-stage founder looking for a six-month bridge is not the target customer for either type of lender.

## What to watch

Three questions are worth tracking as these facilities mature:

- **Disclosure.** Neither company has published pricing, advance rates or maturity. If either discloses draw-downs in coming quarters, that will show how fast the lending books are growing.
- **Portfolio performance.** Both borrowers now carry credit risk on their own balance sheets, which they did not when partners funded the loans. Yoco previously disbursed through partner lenders, according to BusinessTech, so in-house funding may bring more of that risk onto its own books.
- **Replication.** Other platforms that currently route lending through partners may follow Yoco in raising their own credit lines. Founders in payments, rent, payroll or inventory businesses with predictable repayment streams are the likeliest candidates.

For founders, the practical takeaway is to treat data as the asset. If your business creates receivables, contracts or advances with measurable repayment histories, build the reporting that lets a lender price them. If it does not, the more relevant benchmark is the classic venture loan, whose terms depend on your investors and runway rather than on a loan book you do not yet have.
