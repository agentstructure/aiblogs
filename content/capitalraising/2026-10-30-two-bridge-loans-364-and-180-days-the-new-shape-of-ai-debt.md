## Two loans, one pattern

On October 22, two unrelated companies announced large secured loans with unusually short fuses. Nebius (NBIS) disclosed a [$3.5 billion senior secured facility from Citi (C)](https://nebius.com/newsroom/nebius-secures-3-5-billion-financing-facility-with-citi) with a 364-day maturity. The same day, Atlas Energy Solutions (AESI) said it had closed a [$424 million committed senior secured bridge facility](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001984060/000110465926117979/tm2628081d1_ex99-1.htm) from Goldman Sachs (GS) and Barclays (BCS), with a 180-day initial maturity.

The borrowers differ. Nebius builds GPU capacity for AI customers. Atlas is buying power generation equipment for data center projects. The structure is the same: a large, senior, secured loan sized to a near-term build, designed to be refinanced rather than repaid from operations.

For founders and CFOs raising debt, the useful question is not whether AI infrastructure is hot. It is what these terms reveal about how lenders price a short-dated bridge, and what a borrower must have in hand to get one.

## What Nebius signed

According to the company's release, Citi acted as mandated lead arranger, agent and security agent. Pricing starts at Term SOFR plus 2.75% a year and can rise to a maximum of 3.50%. Proceeds go to data center development, GPU procurement and general corporate purposes.

The release also tells you how Nebius sees the loan. COO Ophir Nave described it as providing "additional financing to support our near-term capacity build-out," and said the company has other sources available, including asset-backed financing tied to more than $40 billion of long-term, investment-grade customer contracts. In other words, the 364-day facility is a placeholder for cheaper, longer paper secured against signed revenue.

That fits the sequence laid out in a [TIKR analysis](https://www.tikr.com/blog/nebius-secures-3-5-billion-from-citi-as-it-shifts-from-converts-to-secured-debt). It reports that Nebius raised about $775 million in July in a contract-backed loan at Term SOFR plus 2.50% maturing in 2030, then closed $5.75 billion of convertible notes in August, and that roughly $10 billion of convertibles remain outstanding. The Citi facility is the company's second contract-backed financing. TIKR is a commentary source, so treat its read as one view, but the loan terms it cites match the company's own release.

TIKR also flags the obvious risk: a 364-day term means refinancing arrives quickly, possibly into a market that has recently been tougher on neocloud credit. It notes a Q3 report due November 12.

## What Atlas signed

Atlas's terms come from its own filing. The facility is a delayed draw term loan, with $171 million drawn at closing and the rest available as equipment payments come due. Interest is Adjusted Term SOFR (or Adjusted Daily Simple SOFR) plus 2.50%, stepping up by 0.50% six months after signing. The initial 180-day maturity can be extended once, by 184 days, but only if energy supply agreements are signed for the projects and lenders consent. Atlas can also request up to $286 million of incremental commitments, subject to lender consent.

The credit support matters more than the headline rate. The facility funds power generation and balance-of-plant equipment for two data center projects with first power expected in late 2027. It is tied to two cost reimbursement agreements announced September 25, under which Atlas's customer backstops the equipment purchases and the related bridge facility. CEO John Turner said the customer's "willingness to backstop the purchase" shows its conviction, and that the structure lets Atlas order long-lead items well ahead of commissioning.

So the lender is not underwriting a speculative project. It is lending against a counterparty commitment, with extension rights that depend on further contracts being signed. The press release does not describe the reimbursement terms in detail, so outsiders cannot judge how much of the exposure the customer truly absorbs.

## The pricing signal

Set the two side by side. Nebius starts at 2.75% over SOFR with a ceiling at 3.50%. Atlas starts at 2.50% over and steps up by 0.50% after six months. Both are floating-rate, both are senior and secured, and both charge more for time. Atlas builds that in as an explicit step-up, and Nebius builds it in as a range.

That is the lender's way of saying a bridge is cheap only if it is short. The step-up is a built-in incentive to refinance, and the extension condition at Atlas ties more time to progress on contracts. These are spreads a borrower earns by having signed customers and tangible collateral.

For a smaller company, the transferable lesson is about sequencing. Both borrowers lined up the long-term story (contracted revenue, an investment-grade or backstopping customer) first, then borrowed short against it. A bridge works when the takeout is credible. Without a takeout, the same loan becomes a maturity wall.

## What this means for smaller raises

Few readers are raising billions, but the mechanics scale down. Venture debt, revenue-based financing and equipment lines all turn on the same three questions: what is the collateral or contracted cash flow, when does the loan mature relative to the milestone it funds, and what happens if the milestone slips?

Three practical points follow from these deals.

- **Match the term to the milestone.** Atlas borrowed for equipment that arrives well before first power in late 2027, and built an extension option around the contracts that would let it stay in the loan longer. Ask your lender what extension, if any, is available and what it depends on.
- **Price the step-up.** A rate that rises after six months is a cost you should model at the date it bites, not at signing.
- **Document the backstop.** Atlas's loan leans on a customer commitment. If your facility depends on a contract, a purchase order or a parent guarantee, expect the lender to diligence that document more than your projections.

## What to watch

Three things will show whether short-dated secured bridges are a durable tool or a late-cycle habit.

First, whether Nebius delivers the longer-dated, asset-backed financing it says is available, and at what spread relative to the 2.75% starting point on the bridge. Its November 12 report is the next checkpoint.

Second, whether Atlas signs the energy supply agreements that unlock its extension, and whether it taps the $286 million incremental option. Both are visible in its future filings.

Third, whether other borrowers follow. A [3V digest of that week's AI infrastructure financings](https://3v.org/ai-infrastructure-financing-digest-october-22-2026-shows-short-debt-funding-long-bets/) grouped these deals together under the theme of short debt funding long bets. That is one outlet's framing, but the two primary documents above support the core of it.

None of this is a recommendation about either company's securities. It is a read on how credit is being structured, which is the part founders and finance teams can borrow from when they sit down with their own lenders.
