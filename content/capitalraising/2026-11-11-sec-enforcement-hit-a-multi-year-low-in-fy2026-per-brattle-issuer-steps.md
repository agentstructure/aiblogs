## The numbers

The SEC's fiscal year 2026 ended on September 30, and the agency has not yet published its own official count of enforcement actions. The best available estimate comes from the Brattle Group, an economics consultancy that tallies cases from SEC press releases, litigation releases and administrative proceedings.

Brattle's figure is [308 actions for the full year](https://www.cfodive.com/news/sec-enforcement-actions-sink-57-8-year-average-brattle-group-atkins-gensler-law-fraud/833648/), which CFO Dive reports is 57% below the average for fiscal 2018 onward. [The D&O Diary](https://www.dandodiary.com/2026/11/articles/securities-enforcement/reduced-sec-enforcement-activity-in-the-2026-fiscal-year/), a securities-litigation blog, describes 308 as the lowest annual number since at least FY2018.

The longer history shows how far the docket has dropped. In an [August Brattle note](https://www.brattle.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Q3-Numbers-Refine-Picture-of-SEC-Enforcement-Trajectory.pdf), the firm said the SEC's own annual results ranged from 583 to 862 actions between fiscal 2018 and fiscal 2024, then fell to 456 in fiscal 2025. A different count, from a Covington memo cited by the D&O Diary, looks only at stand-alone actions and finds 232 in FY2026, against 303 in FY2025 and 431 in FY2024.

Two caveats matter. First, these are third-party estimates, and the SEC's official tally may differ. Second, the year was lumpy: the D&O Diary reports 163 actions in the second half against 69 in the first, and CFO Dive says filings rose substantially in September. Brattle's Jan Jindra said the annual total still stayed ["far below historical levels"](https://www.cfodive.com/news/sec-enforcement-actions-sink-57-8-year-average-brattle-group-atkins-gensler-law-fraud/833648/), according to CFO Dive.

## Why the docket shrank

The sources point to several causes. Brattle cited low staffing and the record-long government shutdown at the start of the fiscal year, according to CFO Dive, which also reports a Government Accountability Office finding of an 18% SEC staff cut through September 2025. Margaret Ryan resigned as enforcement director in March, and David Woodcock took the job in May.

There is also a deliberate shift in philosophy. The D&O Diary says Chair Paul Atkins said in a June 2026 speech that the agency is moving away from "regulation by enforcement" and toward fraud, market manipulation and abuses of trust, rather than case counts. Brattle's Q3 note describes a program that is smaller but concentrated around "traditional areas such as market abuse and individual misconduct."

## What it means for issuers

Securities offerings are still a visible part of the docket. According to the D&O Diary's category table, offering cases fell from 88 in FY2025 to 64 in FY2026, a smaller decline than the overall drop. In Brattle's third-quarter data, offering cases were about 25% of the quarter's actions, alongside investment advisers at roughly 25% as well.

Brattle also found that four categories (securities offerings, issuer reporting and auditing, investment advisers and companies, and insider trading) made up 88% of FY2026 activity, per the D&O Diary, up from 75% in FY2025. In other words, the SEC has fewer cases, but offerings remain one of the places where it looks.

For a founder raising under Rule 506(b), Rule 506(c), Regulation A or Regulation Crowdfunding, the practical reading is about what kind of case the SEC wants to bring. The agency's stated priority is fraud. Misstated revenue, invented customers, undisclosed use of proceeds and fake investor commitments are the fact patterns that fit a fraud-focused enforcement program. Nothing in the sources suggests the antifraud rules have been relaxed, and they apply to every offering whether or not it is exempt from registration.

## The private-litigation gap

The D&O Diary raises a point that issuers should not skip: a quieter SEC does not mean no one is watching. The post says that historically at least 20% of securities class actions have followed SEC enforcement actions, and that with less SEC activity plaintiffs' firms may have to run their own investigations. It also notes at least 15 market-manipulation class actions filed in 2026 even as SEC market-manipulation cases fell, which it calls incongruent.

That cuts two ways. Fewer SEC cases may mean fewer ready-made roadmaps for plaintiffs. But the post suggests initiatives such as mandatory arbitration bylaws for IPO companies and optional semiannual reporting could shift more of the burden to private enforcement. For private-company issuers the lesson is simpler: investors who lose money can still sue under state law and federal antifraud provisions, and your offering documents are the first exhibit.

## Practical steps

None of this is legal advice, and issuers should consult securities counsel. As a matter of process, though, a lighter enforcement year is a poor reason to loosen controls. Reasonable habits include:

- Keep a written record backing every number in a deck, data room or Reg CF campaign page, including revenue, user counts and customer names.
- Match what you tell investors in conversation to what is in the offering documents.
- Track how each dollar raised is used against the stated plan, and update investors if the plan changes.
- Confirm that you have complied with your exemption's conditions, such as filings, investor verification for Rule 506(c) and ongoing reports for Reg CF and Reg A issuers.

## What to watch next

Three items are worth tracking. First, the SEC's official FY2026 enforcement results, which will show whether its numbers line up with Brattle's estimate. The FY2025 results showed 456 actions, per the Brattle note above, so the comparison will be easy to check once the agency publishes.

Second, the accredited-investor review. In [Release No. 33-11446](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/33-11446.pdf), the SEC asked for comment on designating CPA licenses in good standing as qualifying for accredited investor status, with comments due on or before December 4, 2026. Companion notices cover other credentials. A wider pool of eligible investors would change who issuers can approach under Regulation D.

Third, whether the second-half rebound continues into fiscal 2027. If September's surge was a one-off year-end push, the docket stays small. If it reflects a rebuilt pipeline, the next count could look different.

For issuers, the working assumption should be that the SEC is looking for fraud, not footnote errors, and that the people who can sue you are not limited to the SEC.
