## What happened

Kalshi, the New York prediction-market exchange, [announced on October 29](https://news.kalshi.com/p/kalshi-series-g-40-billion-valuation) a $1 billion Series G at a $40 billion post-money valuation. Wellington and Sequoia co-led. Ten other investors joined, including Baillie Gifford, Stripes, Andreessen Horowitz, Coatue, Morgan Stanley Investment Management, CapitalG, Hudson River Trading and Balyasny Asset Management.

The round landed in the same week as a Utah criminal case. The Utah Attorney General's Office [charged KalshiEx LLC and co-founders Tarek Mansour and Luana Lopes Lara](https://www.fox13now.com/news/politics/utah-levels-criminal-charges-against-kalshi-and-some-of-its-executives) with one count of second-degree felony pattern of unlawful activity, 12 counts of online gambling promotion (a third-degree felony) and one count of general gambling promotion. [Sports Betting Dime reported the charges on October 28](https://www.sportsbettingdime.com/news/industry/utah-criminal-charges-kalshi/), the day before the funding news. Kalshi called them "a crazy overreach" and said it would fight them in court, as it did in Arizona.

For anyone raising capital, the pairing is the story. A company took a billion dollars at a near-doubled price while a state was prosecuting its founders. Investors did not treat the legal risk as a reason to wait or to cut the price.

## The step-up in numbers

Kalshi's [May 7 Series F](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260507526629/en/Kalshi-Raises-$1-Billion-at-a-$22-Billion-Valuation-as-Institutional-Adoption-Accelerates) was also $1 billion, at $22 billion. The new round is the same check size at a valuation about 82% higher, arriving under six months later. Put differently, the second $1 billion bought a smaller slice of the company: roughly 2.5% at the new price against roughly 4.5% in May, going by the post-money figures. That is simple arithmetic on the announced valuations, not a disclosed ownership figure.

Kalshi's own post supplies the metrics it wants investors to underwrite:

- Annualized trading volume above $1 trillion and more than 30 million accounts created.
- More than $1 billion in non-sports annualized revenue.
- More than 75% of prediction-market trading volume, by the company's own measure.
- About $83 billion of volume in perpetual futures and over 500,000 traders since that product launched a few months ago.
- Around 500 institutional entities active in September, with non-market-maker entities making up about 25% of exchange volume.

These are company-reported figures and none is audited in the announcement. The pattern is still useful. The pitch has moved from retail sports contracts to a diversified exchange with commodities, crypto, politics, international retail and an institutional book. Kalshi says its commodities line produced more than $100 million in annualized trading fees within three months, and that international retail is approaching $100 million in annualized fees one year after launching in more than 140 countries.

## Where the money goes

The use of proceeds reads like an exchange operator's, not a consumer app's. Kalshi says it will spend on institutional sales, distribution and product for hedge funds, prop trading firms and financial institutions. It plans to launch indices, single-stock perpetual futures and compute markets, with a partnership with S&P to build indices on prediction markets. Expansion focuses on Latin America and Europe.

The distribution list matters as much as the product list. Kalshi names Interactive Brokers, Marex, Clear Street, Cantor Fitzgerald, S&P, Nasdaq, Stripe, Public.com, Plus500, Wealthsimple, Alpaca, PayPal and Block as partners. A company that is routing through that many regulated intermediaries is making a bet that its legal fate is tied to the broader financial system, which is a different argument from "we are a gambling app that found a loophole."

## The regulatory discount that did not show up

Founders often assume that open litigation compresses a valuation. This round is a counterexample, with caveats. Kalshi has a record to point to: Arizona filed criminal charges in March, and the company says it beat them. Utah's case has no such outcome yet, and Kalshi's statement only promises to fight it. If the Utah case moves forward, expect it to be priced in later rounds, a tender offer or an IPO filing, not in this one.

For companies in contested regulatory territory, the lesson is narrower than "ignore the lawsuits." Investors here paid for demonstrated demand and revenue breadth, and for a story in which growth increasingly comes from lines beyond sports. Kalshi's $1 billion figure is explicitly non-sports revenue. If your regulatory exposure sits in your largest revenue line, you should not expect the same treatment.

## A second data point: Bedrock Robotics

[Crunchbase's weekly list for October 24 to 30](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/biggest-rounds-startups-physical-ai-predictions-robotics-rebuild/) put Kalshi second, behind Re:Build's $2.1 billion round that this site covered earlier in the week, and Bedrock Robotics third. Bedrock, a San Francisco maker of autonomy kits for construction equipment, raised a $600 million Series C at a $4.1 billion post-money valuation, co-led by CapitalG and Emergence Capital.

[The Next Web reports](https://thenextweb.com/news/bedrock-robotics-600m-series-c-4-1bn-valuation) that the round was announced October 26 and follows a $270 million Series B in February at a $1.75 billion valuation. That is a step-up of roughly 2.3 times in about eight months. Total funding is nearly $1 billion. Its autonomous excavators have been doing paid work on live job sites with no operator since mid-August, and the customer base has more than doubled since 2025. Revenue was not disclosed.

Bedrock and Kalshi share one trait and one investor. CapitalG appears in both rounds. And both raised on a milestone that is easy to state: paid autonomous work on real job sites in one case, trillion-dollar annualized volume in the other. Late-stage investors are still willing to pay up quickly, but they are paying for something that has already happened.

## What to watch

- **Utah's next move.** Watch for a federal court challenge or a ruling on whether federal law preempts state gambling enforcement, the argument Kalshi says it won in Arizona.
- **Institutional share.** The 25% non-market-maker figure is the number that tests the exchange thesis. If it rises, the $40 billion price has support. If it stalls, the round looks more like a bet on sports volume than Kalshi's post admits.
- **Follow-on pricing.** A step-up this steep usually sets the bar for rivals' fundraising and for secondary sales. Check whether competitors can show comparable volume before citing Kalshi as a comp in your own deck.
- **Your own ratio.** Founders can run the same dilution math on their next round. Raise the same dollars at a higher price and you give up less; Kalshi sold roughly 2.5% for its $1 billion and Bedrock roughly 15% for its $600 million, going by announced post-money valuations.
