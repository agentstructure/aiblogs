## What the SEC proposed

Most coverage of securities regulation for issuers this month has focused on private markets: accredited investor definitions, Regulation CF caps, Regulation D. A bigger pending change sits on the public side, and it matters to any founder who expects to be listed within a few years. On May 19, 2026, the SEC proposed two companion rulemakings that Chairman Paul Atkins [described as the foundation of his "Make IPOs Great Again" agenda](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/atkins-statement-on-proposing-releases-for-enhancement-of-emerging-growth-company-accommodations-and-simplification-of-filer-status-for-reporting-companies-and-registered-offering-reform-051926).

The first, Release No. 33-11418, is [registered offering reform](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/proposed/2026/33-11418.pdf). The second, Release No. 33-11419, simplifies the categories of public-company filers. Neither is a final rule. I found no report of an adoption vote, so everything below describes a proposal that could change before it takes effect.

The headline item is Form S-3, the short-form registration statement that lets a company sell securities quickly off a shelf. According to [Dechert's summary](https://www.dechert.com/knowledge/onpoint/2026/5/sec-proposes-overhaul-of-reporting-filer-status-and-registered-o.html), the proposal would eliminate both the one-year reporting seasoning requirement and the $75 million public float requirement for unlimited primary offerings. It would also remove the transaction requirements, so any eligible issuer could use S-3 for primary or secondary offerings, other than exchange offers and business combinations.

## Why the baby shelf matters

Today, a company with less than $75 million in public float that does use S-3 for a primary offering is held to the "baby shelf" limit in General Instruction I.B.6: it can sell no more than one-third of its public float in any rolling 12 months. For a company with a $30 million float, that is a $10 million ceiling a year, however good the opportunity or bad the cash position. Dechert reports that the proposal would eliminate that limit.

The SEC's own estimate, as relayed by Dechert, is that more than 60% more issuers would become eligible for unlimited shelf offerings on S-3. The agency's rationale, in Atkins' statement, is that eligibility should rest mainly on timely SEC reporting rather than on size or age, and that many public-offering rules have not been updated in over twenty years.

The reporting condition stays. Issuers would need to be current and timely in their Exchange Act filings over the preceding 12 months, with a new grace period allowing one late filing if it is made within seven calendar days of the due date, per Dechert.

The proposal also adds exclusions for higher-risk issuers. Dechert lists blank check issuers, certain shell companies, penny stock issuers, and issuers affected by bad actor or enforcement-order triggers. Former SPACs would be carved out of the three-year shell lookback after a de-SPAC.

## A new tier replaces the WKSI label

The second change is about who gets the fastest tools. Automatic shelf registration is currently reserved for well-known seasoned issuers (WKSIs), a category defined largely by size. The proposal would create a new Rule 405 category, the "exchange-listed issuer" (ELI), for domestic issuers that meet the proposed S-3 requirements and have at least one class of common equity listed on a national exchange.

Within that group, a "seasoned" ELI is one that has been subject to Exchange Act reporting for at least twelve calendar months. Only seasoned ELIs would get automatic shelf registration. All ELIs would pick up other benefits now limited to WKSIs: greater communications flexibility, pay-as-you-go registration fees, and the ability to add securities through a post-effective amendment. Dechert says WKSI status would be retained only for foreign private issuers.

The SEC estimates that more than 200% more issuers would qualify for the full set of enhanced benefits. In Atkins' framing, the point is that listing and reporting history, not market capitalization, become the gatekeepers.

## The filer-status companion

The companion proposal moves several thresholds that have been fixed for years. Per Dechert, it would raise the large accelerated filer threshold from $700 million to $2 billion of public float, a figure the SEC notes has not been updated since 2005. It would eliminate the accelerated filer and smaller reporting company categories, leaving two tiers, and every new registrant would start as a non-accelerated filer with access to scaled disclosure.

Atkins' statement says about 52% of companies now receive some form of disclosure scaling, and the proposal would extend those benefits to 81%. The remaining companies, which face the heaviest requirements, account for roughly 93.5% of total public market float. Dechert also reports that the SEC estimates 26.7% more current registrants would qualify as non-accelerated filers exempt from the Sarbanes-Oxley Section 404(b) auditor attestation requirement, and that companies with $35 million or less in total assets would get longer deadlines: 120 days for Form 10-K and 50 days for Form 10-Q.

One more piece draws less attention: blue sky preemption. Dechert reports the proposal would make securities sold in any registered offering "covered securities," ending state registration and qualification while leaving states their anti-fraud authority. That is especially significant for non-traded BDCs and non-traded REITs.

## What to watch and how to prepare

Three things are worth tracking. First, timing. Dechert lists comment deadlines of July 20 for the filer-status proposal and July 27 for offering reform, so the record is closed, and the next step is a Commission vote on final text. I found no date for that vote. Law-firm commentary such as [Goodwin's June alert](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/alerts-lifesciences-cm-sec-proposal-eliminate-baby-shelf-limitations) notes there is no certainty about the final form or timing.

Second, the follow-on agenda. Atkins said future proposals, including Regulation S-K reform with materiality as its guiding principle, will build on these two.

Third, how the market reacts if the baby shelf goes. Easier access to S-3 cuts both ways for small listed companies. It gives a company with a thin float a faster route to raise capital without a full registration statement, but it also makes repeated issuance easier, and existing holders bear the dilution. Investors and boards will want to see how companies use the flexibility.

For founders and finance teams, a few practical steps do not depend on the final text:

- Treat filing discipline as an asset. Under the proposal, a clean, on-time 12-month record is the main entry ticket to S-3, and one missed deadline beyond the seven-day grace could close the door.
- Model your capital plan under both regimes. If you are a listed company under $75 million in float, compare the one-third-of-float cap with an uncapped shelf, and decide now what governance limits you would put on usage.
- Check which tier you would land in. Your filer status, audit attestation cost and disclosure load could all change if the thresholds move.
- Talk to securities counsel before the final rule, not after, so you can pre-negotiate shelf documentation with underwriters.

This is information, not legal or investment advice. The proposal is long, and the details of the conditions and exclusions are in the [release itself](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/proposed/2026/33-11418.pdf).
