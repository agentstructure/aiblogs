## What the Cooley data says

Most coverage of venture data focuses on how much money moved. For a founder holding a term sheet, the more useful question is what the paper looks like. Law firm Cooley publishes a quarterly report built from the deals its own lawyers handled, and its [Q3 2026 data page](https://www.cooleygo.com/data/) gives a clean read on terms.

Some framing first. The figures cover Cooley-handled venture financings in the third quarter of 2026, not the whole market. Cooley says it handled 162 reported financings representing $10.7 billion in invested capital. That is a meaningful sample of venture-backed companies, but it skews toward the clients a large venture law firm serves. Treat the percentages as a benchmark, not a census.

Within that sample, the headline terms were conventional. According to Cooley, a "1x" liquidation preference appeared in 97.5% of deals, and 98.1% provided for nonparticipating preferred stock. Accruing dividends showed up in approximately 3.1% of deals, and redemption provisions appeared in 0.6%.

The price side looked healthy too. Cooley reports that up rounds accounted for 87.5% of Q3 deals, down rounds were 7.7% and flat rounds were 4.8%.

Then there is the outlier. Cooley says pay-to-play provisions appeared in 11.1% of reported deals in the quarter. That is a term most founders associate with distress, and it showed up in a market where most rounds were priced up.

## Why the 1x number matters in negotiation

A 1x nonparticipating preference means an investor chooses between getting its money back or converting to common and sharing in the upside, not both. When nearly every deal in a sample looks like that, it becomes the default you can point to.

If a lead proposes something heavier, such as a multiple above 1x, participation or cumulative dividends, you have a data point for the conversation. The Cooley figures say those features were rare in this sample. They do not say the term is unreasonable in every case, and a company with weak leverage may still accept one. But the burden of explanation shifts to the investor.

The reverse also applies. If your term sheet is plain, do not spend negotiating capital fighting over a term nobody else is fighting over. Cooley's 97.5% suggests the preference multiple is rarely where the real negotiation sits.

## What pay-to-play does

A pay-to-play provision ties an investor's preferred rights to continued investment. Morrison Foerster describes these provisions as ones that "usually require existing holders of preferred stock to purchase, on a pro rata basis, additional shares" in a future financing. An investor that does not reinvest can have some or all of its preferred shares converted to common stock or a more junior class of preferred. Losing preferred status can mean losing a liquidation preference, participation rights in later rounds, preferred voting rights and the right to elect a preferred board designee, [per the firm's guide](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/231116-nvca-pay-to-play-provisions).

[AngelList](https://learn.angellist.com/articles/pay-to-play-provision) adds the carrot version. A pull-through or pull-up lets participating investors swap existing preferred shares for a new class with better terms, such as a higher liquidation preference, which leaves non-participants subordinate to that class. It also notes that companies typically turn to pay-to-play provisions when they are struggling to raise capital, and that they fell out of favor in the strong funding environment of the early 2020s.

For a company, the appeal is plain: the provision pressures existing backers to keep funding or accept a worse position. For an existing investor who is not planning to follow on, it is a cost. For a founder, the tradeoff is relationship risk. AngelList cautions that a pay-to-play can strain relationships if it feels punitive.

## Why it could be showing up now

Cooley's page does not explain the 11.1%, and I will not pretend to know the cause. A few explanations are plausible, none confirmed by the data. One is that the market is bifurcated. Foley & Lardner, citing Crunchbase, reports that roughly one third of global venture capital in Q3 went to just 27 companies raising billion-dollar-plus rounds. When capital concentrates at the top, companies outside that group may find that existing investors need a structural nudge to keep writing checks.

Another is that pay-to-play terms are being used in well-funded rounds to align insiders, rather than as a rescue device. A third is a small-sample effect: in a quarter with 162 deals, a handful of transactions can move a percentage. That is a reason to watch the next two reports before calling it a trend.

Whatever the cause, the practical point stands. A term that used to signal trouble is not rare enough to ignore when you read a term sheet.

## A checklist for your next term sheet

None of this is legal advice, and your counsel should read your actual documents. But the data points to a short list of questions worth asking before you sign.

- **Is the preference 1x and nonparticipating?** If not, ask what the investor is giving in exchange, and compare against the 97.5% and 98.1% figures above.
- **Are there accruing dividends or redemption rights?** Both were uncommon in Cooley's sample, at roughly 3.1% and 0.6% of deals.
- **Does the term sheet include pay-to-play language?** Find out whether it is a conversion penalty, a pull-through reward or both. AngelList says the consequences differ, so identify which one you are dealing with early in the round.
- **Who is affected?** A pay-to-play binds your existing investors as much as your new one. Ask whether your current backers have seen the language and have the capacity to follow on.
- **What happens at the next round?** Terms you accept now set the floor for the next negotiation. A pull-through that lifts one class's preference can crowd later investors.
- **How does the price compare with your cap table?** Cooley's data shows most rounds in the sample priced up. If yours is flat or down, expect more structure around it.

## What to watch next

Two things. First, Cooley's Q4 2026 report, which will show whether pay-to-play stays elevated or drops back. Second, whether other law firms that publish term data, such as Fenwick and Wilson Sonsini, report the same pattern in their samples. A single firm's number is a signal. Two or three firms pointing the same way is a trend.

For founders, the takeaway is modest and practical. The economics of a standard priced round remain standard. The details around who must keep funding, and what happens to those who do not, are where this quarter's data suggests you should read more closely.
