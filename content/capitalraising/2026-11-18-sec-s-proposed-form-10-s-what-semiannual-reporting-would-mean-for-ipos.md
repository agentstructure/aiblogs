## The proposal, in one paragraph

On May 5, 2026, the SEC proposed letting reporting companies [replace three quarterly Form 10-Q filings with one semiannual report on a new Form 10-S](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/atkins-statement-proposing-release-semiannual-reporting-050526). Chairman Paul Atkins framed it as part of his "Make IPOs Great Again" agenda and as a way to encourage companies to go and stay public. It is an option, not a mandate. The comment period [closed July 6, 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-05-11-the-secs-semiannual-reporting-proposal-fare-thee-well-quarterly-reporting), and as of this writing no final rule has been adopted.

For founders and CFOs raising capital, the interesting question is not whether quarterly reporting disappears. It is what the choice does to the next financing: the IPO, the shelf takedown, the credit facility.

## How the election would work

Based on [Orrick's summary of the release](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/05/SEC-Proposes-Amendments-to-Permit-Semiannual-Reporting-by-Reporting-Companies), Form 10-S would carry the same narrative disclosures and financial information as a 10-Q, but cover a six-month period. The financial statements must follow U.S. GAAP and be reviewed by an auditor, though they need not be audited. The deadline would be 40 or 45 days after the first semiannual period ends, depending on filer status, which matches today's 10-Q deadline. The second half-year would sit inside the Form 10-K.

The election would be annual, made with a checkbox on the 10-K cover page. Quarterly is the default, so a company that wants semiannual reporting has to re-elect each year, and the choice binds it for that fiscal year. Similar checkboxes would appear on the cover pages of Forms S-1, S-3, S-4, S-11 and Form 10.

Two things do not change. The SEC's footnote says the proposal [would not affect the frequency of a company's earnings call and earnings release](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/atkins-statement-proposing-release-semiannual-reporting-050526), and Orrick notes that the Form 8-K changes are only technical amendments to Item 2.02 to reference semiannual periods. A company that skips the 10-Q can still talk to investors every quarter if it wants to.

## What it means for a company heading to an IPO

This is where the proposal is most relevant to this audience. Per [Cooley's analysis](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-05-11-the-secs-semiannual-reporting-proposal-fare-thee-well-quarterly-reporting), a private company in an IPO could make its initial election on the registration statement and change it until the registration statement becomes effective. After that it could not switch midyear. The first Form 10-S would be due on the later of 45 days after effectiveness or the date it would otherwise have been due.

Cooley also gives an example of the effect on staleness. A registration statement filed as late as August 13 for a nonreporting, calendar-year company that elects semiannual reporting would not require any interim financial statements under proposed Rule 3-01(c)(2). In practice that could mean a longer window in which an issuer can go to market on year-end numbers alone.

The election is a signal, too. A newly public company that opts for semiannual reporting is telling investors something about how it will communicate. Investors who buy in at the IPO will be underwriting that choice along with the business.

## Where the friction is: lenders, underwriters and blackouts

Cooley flags several practical snags that matter more to issuers than the headline benefit of fewer filings.

**Credit agreements.** Companies with debt covenants requiring quarterly information may find that quarterly reporting better serves their needs. Anyone with a revolver or term loan should check what the financial reporting covenant actually says before assuming a semiannual election is free.

**Underwriters and comfort letters.** Underwriters may ask for auditor review of more recent interim financials than those in the last semiannual or annual report in order to give negative assurance comfort. Cooley's conclusion is that companies with near-term capital raising needs may need to keep reporting quarterly, depending on how underwriting practice adapts.

**Shelf and S-8 updating.** Registration statements that incorporate Exchange Act reports would be updated twice a year instead of four times. Cooley notes this applies to shelf and resale registrations and to Form S-8 employee equity plans. Fewer updates mean fewer refreshes, but also a thinner disclosure record between them.

**Trading windows.** Longer gaps between disclosures could require longer blackout periods under insider trading policies, and holding material nonpublic information for longer raises the risk of inadvertent disclosure under Regulation FD. Cooley also points out that Rule 10b5-1 cooling-off periods tie to when financial results are disclosed, so plan timing for insiders would shift too.

**Peer comparison.** Investors use quarterly peers' more frequent disclosures as signals of industry trends, so a semiannual filer's stock could move on competitors' results before the company has said anything itself.

## The opposition

The proposal drew a lot of pushback. [Skadden reports](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/09/insights-september-2026/sec-semiannual-reporting) that the SEC received over 200,000 comment letters, a record for the agency on a proposed rulemaking, with the vast majority opposed. Retail investors wrote the bulk of them. SIFMA and the Investment Company Institute opposed the change while still supporting lighter regulatory burdens, and the CFA Institute opposed it too. Skadden says NASAA, the state securities regulators, argued the proposal "would raise capital costs, reduce liquidity, weaken investor confidence" and noted that other jurisdictions that adopted semiannual reporting saw no meaningful public market growth.

Support came from the corporate side. According to Skadden, a mega-cap pharmaceutical company said it would elect semiannual reporting if the rule is adopted, eight other pharmaceutical companies jointly voiced support, and Business Roundtable argued that quarterly reporting contributes to short-termism. Skadden adds that most companies have not said whether they would elect it.

Atkins's own statement points to the same trade-off. He lists the costs and management time of quarterly versus semiannual reports as a factor companies would weigh, alongside investor expectations, the possible effect on cost of capital and the possibility of increased research coverage.

## What to watch

The SEC has announced no timetable, per Skadden. Staff will review the comments and recommend a path to the Commission, and any final rule needs a Commission vote. A rule adopted after 200,000 mostly critical letters could well differ from the proposal, so issuers should not plan around the current text.

Practical steps that cost nothing and keep options open:

- Read the financial reporting covenant in every credit agreement and note which require quarterly financials.
- Ask counsel and your underwriter how comfort-letter practice would change if you elected semiannual reporting.
- Decide, before the IPO, whether the board wants quarterly earnings releases regardless of the filing cadence, since the SEC's proposal leaves that untouched.
- Revisit insider trading policies and blackout calendars against a six-month disclosure rhythm.

This is information and commentary, not investment or legal advice. The saving from dropping two filings a year is real, but for a company that expects to borrow or sell stock in the next two years, the fine print on lenders, underwriters and blackout windows may decide the election.
