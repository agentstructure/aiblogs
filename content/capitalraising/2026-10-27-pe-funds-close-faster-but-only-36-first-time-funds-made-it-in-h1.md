## Two different fundraising markets

Headline numbers for private equity fundraising look steadier than they have in years. Law firm Paul Weiss reports in its [Q2 2026 fundraising review](https://www.paulweiss.com/media/05uhvzek/pe_fundraising_at_a_glance_q2_2026.pdf) that global PE funds raised $191.4 billion in the quarter, up 9% from $175.5 billion in Q1 and up 5.5% from $181.4 billion a year earlier. The number of funds closing tells a different story: 284 funds closed in Q2, down 19% from 351 in Q2 2025.

More money across fewer funds is the signature of a market that is open but selective. The data below shows who is getting through, how fast, and who is being left waiting. For founders raising a fund, or CFOs weighing which managers will have capital to back their companies, the split matters more than the total.

Note on sources: Paul Weiss and PitchBook count differently. PitchBook puts Q2 2026 private equity closings at [$165.3 billion](https://pitchbook.brightspotcdn.com/74/45/4f36da844589a606c5cea79f2aeb/q2-2026-global-private-market-fundraising-report.pdf), lower than Paul Weiss's $191.4 billion. Compare figures within a dataset, not across them.

## Funds that close are closing faster

The encouraging part is speed. Paul Weiss puts the average time to final close at 15.7 months in the first half of 2026, down from 19.1 months for full-year 2025. It also reports that 82% of funds closed at or above their target in H1, the highest level in at least six years.

Average fund size is growing too. The average final close was $738 million across all PE, up 15.3% from $640 million a year earlier, and $1.023 billion for buyout funds, up 14.7% from $892 million.

Read together, those figures suggest that when a manager has a clear story and an existing LP base, the process has become more efficient. A faster close also lets a manager return to deploying capital sooner.

There is a survivorship caveat. These averages describe funds that reached a final close. Funds still in the market are not in the sample, and there are plenty of them: Paul Weiss counts 7,739 funds in market, up 29.6% from 5,973 a year earlier, seeking an aggregate $1.052 trillion. A fast average among closers does not mean the typical fundraiser is having a fast process.

## The top of the market takes most of it

Concentration explains much of the gap. According to Paul Weiss, the ten largest final closes accounted for 49% of all capital raised globally in Q2. Buyout funds took 72% of capital, up from 57% in Q2 2025, while growth funds fell to 9% from 16%.

PitchBook's data points the same way over a longer window. It finds that 77% of PE capital raised since 2024 went to funds over $1 billion, compared with 63.6% in 2014-2016. Across all private capital, funds above $1 billion took 78.2% of H1 2026 commitments, up from 59.1% in 2021.

Size and track record, in other words, appear to decide who gets a fast close.

## First-time funds are the exception

The sharpest contrast is at the other end. PitchBook reports that [36 first-time PE funds closed in H1 2026, raising $7.7 billion](https://pitchbook.brightspotcdn.com/74/45/4f36da844589a606c5cea79f2aeb/q2-2026-global-private-market-fundraising-report.pdf). For comparison, the report lists 123 first-time funds and $21.3 billion for all of 2025.

That is a half-year against a full year, so the two figures are not directly comparable. Still, the average first-time fund in H1 raised about $214 million (my arithmetic: $7.7 billion divided by 36), and the fund count would need to roughly triple in the second half to match 2025. Nothing in the report suggests that is under way, though it does not forecast the second half.

Carta's piece on [emerging managers](https://carta.com/data/emerging-managers-fundraising-2026/) supplies context rather than a first-time count. It cites PitchBook data showing the number of PE funds closed fell 44% in 2025 while cash raised dipped 24%, which fits a pattern of fewer, larger funds.

## Why drawdown fundraising is still under pressure

Part of the reason is that LPs have less cash to recycle. Opalesque, [citing PitchBook](https://www.opalesque.com/714886/Private_equity_on_pace_for_another_slow_fundraising488.html), noted in June that fundraising for traditional drawdown vehicles was down for a third year in a row, with managers drawing on existing dry powder instead of raising new commitments.

Dry powder is large but no longer rising. Paul Weiss puts PE dry powder at $2.347 trillion at the end of 2025, about 25% of assets under management, down from 28% six quarters earlier. A lower ratio means managers are putting money to work, which is the precondition for LPs getting distributions and re-upping.

PitchBook adds that a growing share of the industry's net asset value is aging. Almost $5 trillion, or 39% of total NAV across private markets, sits in funds seven or more years old. Capital that is stuck in older funds is capital that cannot back new managers.

## What fundraisers should take from this

First, benchmark against the right peer group. If you are raising a first fund, the 15.7-month average and the 82% at-or-above-target figure describe a population that is mostly established managers. Plan for a longer process and a smaller target than those headlines imply.

Second, expect LPs to weigh your fund against its likely size. With buyout taking 72% of Q2 capital and growth only 9%, growth and early-stage strategies are competing for a thinner slice. Evidence of prior deal flow and a defined LP base carries more weight when the number of funds in market is rising.

Third, if you are a company founder rather than a fund manager, the concentration affects you indirectly. Capital is gathering in a smaller number of large pools, so the investors writing checks to your sector may be fewer and larger than they were a few years ago.

## What to watch next

Three data points will show whether the split is narrowing. The Q3 2026 fundraising reports from PitchBook and Paul Weiss should show whether average time to close holds near 15.7 months as the number of funds in market keeps growing. The count of first-time funds in the second half will show whether H1's 36 was a trough or a trend. And the pace of distributions to LPs will indicate whether older funds begin releasing capital that can be redeployed to new managers.

None of this is a forecast for any particular fund or strategy. It is a description of how the market currently allocates capital, and the data says that allocation has become more uneven even as the average process has become quicker.
