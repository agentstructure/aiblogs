## A lease, not a loan

Most founders raising debt think in two buckets: a term loan against revenue, or venture debt layered on an equity round. This week's biggest debt deal fits neither, and it is worth studying because the structure scales down.

SpaceX (SPCX) told investors on Oct. 26 that it had made a [binding commitment to enter a master finance lease](https://ir.spacex.com/updates/releases-details/2026/SPACEX-SECURES-40-BILLION-IN-THE-LARGEST-GPU-FINANCING-EVER-2026-EnDqGhTAB3) of about $40 billion for GPUs and related equipment. The lessor is an entity managed by Apollo Global Management (APO). The chips are Nvidia (NVDA) Vera Rubin units for SpaceX's terrestrial compute buildout. Goldman Sachs (GS) was sole structuring agent, and BlackRock (BLK), Goldman Sachs Asset Management and KKR (KKR) capital accounts made anchor commitments, per the company.

SpaceX does not borrow money and then buy chips. The Apollo-managed entity buys the hardware and leases it to SpaceX. According to [TechCrunch's report on the filing](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/26/spacex-locks-in-40b-gpu-financing-deal-with-apollo/), each batch of equipment carries its own lease schedule of about five to five and a quarter years, starting as it is delivered. Closing is expected before year-end, with leases beginning in the first half of 2027.

## What the bond sale added

The debt that funds the lessor has now reached the market. [Seeking Alpha reported on Nov. 20](https://seekingalpha.com/news/4675034-spacex-sells-32b-in-ai-chip-debt-above-8-yield-report) that SpaceX sold $32 billion of bonds to fund its AI chip purchases at yields above 8%, and that the deal may serve as a model for similar financings. Seeking Alpha attributes the news to a report; we could not confirm the figure in a primary filing, so treat it as reported.

[Wisevoter's account](https://wisevoter.com/world/2026/11/20/spacex-sells-32-billion-bonds-ai-chips) adds detail that we also could not verify independently. It says the largest note tranche yields 8.259%, against a 5.95% average for US high-grade notes, and that the bonds sit inside a financing of more than $40 billion. It also says the debt was placed off SpaceX's balance sheet and that the bonds were sold in three tranches. Sources differ on the exact sale date, with one pointing to Thursday and another to Friday, so we say only that it happened this week.

Bloomberg's reporting, as [relayed by TIKR](https://www.tikr.com/blog/musk-would-never-lend-money-to-pure-ai-clouds-goldman-is-selling-spacexs-40-billion-gpu-debt), describes the mechanism: a special purpose vehicle managed by Apollo-backed Atlas SP buys the chips and issues the debt, which keeps it off SpaceX's balance sheet. Bloomberg also reported that Goldman led an $8.3 billion senior-secured loan sold to other banks, and that the debt plus a $7 billion lease prepayment fund about $47.3 billion of chips. Those figures rest on that one secondhand account, so read them as indicative.

## Why lenders accept it

The pitch to lenders is that the borrower's promise matters more than the hardware's resale value. Per the same Bloomberg-based account, SpaceX's marketing presentation reportedly says rent is owed regardless of what happens to the equipment, force majeure included. That is the feature that makes a finance lease behave more like a corporate obligation than a pool of collateral.

Credit quality sits behind it. TechCrunch reports that SpaceX's AI segment brought in $7.6 billion of its $14.6 billion third-quarter revenue, and that capital expenditures reached nearly $20 billion, up from $6.9 billion a year earlier. The CFO told investors the company is seeing "less than one year payback periods" on its infrastructure investments. That is a management claim, not an audited result, and it is the assumption the whole structure leans on.

The same article quotes Elon Musk on X saying debt markets have largely shut out companies without major revenue beyond AI data centers, and predicting that "the 200+ neoclouds are going to see guppy-level death and consolidation." That is an opinion from an interested party, but the direction of travel matches what lenders are doing: pricing the borrower first and the chips second.

## The risk nobody can price yet

The weak point is the mismatch between a roughly five-year lease and a chip that may be superseded sooner. Short seller Jim Chanos made that argument about neocloud borrowers in an [interview reported Dec. 1, 2025](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/famed-short-seller-jim-chanos-sees-risks-in-growing-debt-market-backed-by-nvidias-ai-chips-theres-going-to-be-debt-defaults-110013557.html). He noted that Nvidia releases new products roughly every 18 months and said the economics "fall apart" if chips last only three years. The same report says Amazon (AMZN) cut its depreciation schedule for AI chips from six years to five, and that most cloud firms, CoreWeave (CRWV) among them, assume about six years of revenue from the chips.

Chanos was talking about loss-making borrowers, not SpaceX. But the question is the same for any equipment-backed deal: if the asset loses value faster than the lease amortizes, who bears the gap? In SpaceX's structure, the reported answer is the lessee, through rent owed no matter what. [Wisevoter notes](https://wisevoter.com/world/2026/11/20/spacex-sells-32-billion-bonds-ai-chips) that whether the lease structure holds up over its term remains untested.

## What smaller borrowers can take from it

You will not get Apollo and Goldman on a Series A. The structural ideas still travel.

**Match the instrument to the asset.** Hardware-heavy companies can often finance equipment through a lease or equipment line rather than burning equity on it. [Hadrian's $360 million revolving credit facility](https://dealroom.co/news/145088-hadrian-closes-360m-revolving-credit-facility-to-expand-us-manufacturing/), reported by Dealroom on Aug. 14 and led by Morgan Stanley with a group of banks, was earmarked for manufacturing infrastructure, machinery and hardware. Dealroom reports it came about a week after a $1.37 billion Series D. The article does not disclose rate, tenor or collateral.

**Read the "hell or high water" clause.** If rent is owed no matter what happens to the equipment, the lease is a firm obligation on your balance sheet whatever the accounting label. Model it as debt service when you size runway.

**Check the useful life.** Ask the lessor how the lease term compares with the asset's realistic economic life and what happens at the end: purchase option, renewal or return. A term longer than the useful life moves risk to you.

**Look at who sits above you.** In an arrangement with a separate vehicle, lenders get paid from the lessor's cash flows. As a borrower, confirm what happens if the lessor or its funding falls through before delivery.

**Price against the right comparison.** The reported 8.259% for the largest tranche was set for a company with public-market disclosure and anchor investors. A private company with less transparency should expect to pay more, so ask for term sheets from at least two lenders.

## What to watch next

Three things. First, whether the lease closes before year-end as SpaceX expects. Second, whether other large borrowers copy the structure, which Seeking Alpha says it may inspire. Third, how the bonds trade after issue, since secondary pricing will show whether buyers are comfortable with the mismatch between five years of rent and the useful life of the chips.

For founders and CFOs, the takeaway is not that AI hardware debt is cheap or risky in the abstract. It is that lenders in late 2026 are differentiating by structure: who owns the asset, who bears the obsolescence risk, and who owes rent when things go wrong. Know the answers before you sign.
