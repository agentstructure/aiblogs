## Record deals, stalled exits

The newest PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor, covering data through Sept. 30, 2026, tells two stories at once. On the deal side, the [report](https://pitchbook.com/news/reports/q3-2026-pitchbook-nvca-venture-monitor) puts US venture deal value at $515.8 billion year to date across an estimated 14,199 deals, the highest in the dataset and about 44% above the full-year record set in 2021. AI accounts for a record 82.7% of that value.

On the exit side, the same report says the IPO window "has not reopened," even after SpaceX's record $1.7 trillion listing in the second quarter. Distributions to limited partners, the report says, remain "the market's key obstacle."

For founders and fund managers, the gap between those two facts is the most useful thing in the data. Money is flowing into companies at a record pace, but it is not flowing back out to the people who funded earlier rounds. That shapes who can raise a fund, who can raise a round, and on what terms.

## The LP squeeze in one number

The [full report](https://nvca.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Q3-2026-PitchBook-NVCA-Venture-Monitor.pdf) cites EisnerAmper: net cash flow to LPs has been negative by $202 billion since 2022, "even as market value and AUM continued to climb." In plain terms, LPs have contributed far more cash than they have received back, while their paper holdings have kept rising.

Paper gains do not fund new commitments. Pension funds, endowments and family offices need distributions to re-up, and the report says it does not expect LP preference to shift structurally "until LPs see real relief on the distribution side."

The fundraising numbers show where that preference has landed. Venture funds have raised $108.5 billion across 699 funds this year, up 38.6% from $78.3 billion in all of 2025. But the distribution of that money is lopsided:

- Funds of $500 million or more took 78.1% of capital raised while making up only 6% of funds closed.
- Established managers (fund four and above) took a record 88.2% of capital.
- Andreessen Horowitz closed $23.8 billion across multiple funds, more than 20% of all US venture fundraising this year.
- The median fund size is $10 million, against an average of $165.4 million. That gap shows how much of the total sits in a few very large vehicles.

First-time managers raised $4.9 billion across 81 funds. Emerging firms are still closing funds (211 have closed at least one through Q3), but the capital is a small slice of a bigger pie.

We covered the top-three concentration in H1 earlier this month. The new data suggests it is a structural response to the distribution drought, not a one-quarter blip. LPs short on cash are backing managers with a record of returning it.

## Where the exits are, and what they paid

The exit data is lumpy. The report shows the Q3 total was dominated by one deal: a $60 billion all-stock purchase of Cursor, which the report calls the second-largest M&A deal involving a venture-backed company and which made up 53.1% of Q3 exit value. Strip it out and the rest of the quarter looks far smaller, since the Cursor deal was more than half of the total.

Further down the size scale, the picture is harsher. Airtable sold for $1.3 billion, down from a previous $11.7 billion valuation. Miro is expected to close in Q4 at $1.4 billion, against a $17.5 billion Series C. By our arithmetic from the report's figures, those are discounts of about 89% and 92%. PitchBook's summary describes both as discounts of nearly 90%.

Those are not distress sales by obscure companies. They are well-known software businesses selling for a fraction of their last private marks. For anyone holding a late-stage cap table, that is the clearest sign of what happens when a company cannot wait out the IPO window.

Meanwhile the unicorn count keeps growing: the report counts 992 active unicorns worth a combined $5.7 trillion, including 179 companies that reached unicorn status in 2026. Every one of them is a claim on the same scarce set of exit routes. The report also points to a backlog of companies that "need to, or should have, gone public in the past few years."

## Concentration on the deal side too

Capital is clustering in rounds as well as funds. According to the report, $100 million-plus deals were about 5% of Q3 deal count but 75% of Q3 deal value. The largest Q3 deal was Databricks at $5 billion. Quarterly deal value fell to $98.4 billion in Q3, down about 40% from Q2, which shows how much the totals swing on a handful of giant rounds.

AI's share of quarterly deal value was 88.4% in Q1, 84.1% in Q2 and 65.9% in Q3. AI is still the majority of value in every quarter, but its share dipped in Q3 while its share of deal count was 44.9%. In other words, a bit more than four in ten deals are AI deals, but they carry most of the dollars.

Valuations tell a similar story of a two-speed market. The report's median pre-money figures (our read of the report's chart, which does not label every year clearly) put 2026 medians at roughly $60.3 million for seed, $187.0 million for Series A, $500.0 million for Series B and $1.72 billion for Series D and later. The report's 2025 comparison points are roughly $48.1 million, $140.0 million, $308.5 million and $831.9 million. Treat the exact mapping with care, but the direction, with later-stage medians rising fastest, is consistent with the rest of the report.

## What to watch next

Several things matter for anyone raising capital in Q4 and early 2027:

- **Fund managers:** LP diligence is likely to center on realized returns and distributions. Track record in DPI, not just marked IRR, is what the report says LPs are rewarding. Emerging managers should expect to be asked how they plan to return cash on a shorter timeline.
- **Founders:** headline valuations are strong in hot categories, but Airtable and Miro show that some well-known companies are being sold at steep discounts to last-round prices. If your last round was priced in 2021 or 2022, plan for the possibility that your next event is below it, and negotiate terms with that in mind.
- **Late-stage companies:** the backlog is real. The report treats a reopened IPO window as the main thing that would change LP behavior. Watch Q4 pricing and aftermarket performance for signals.
- **Data caveats:** the report's own figures are estimates, and quarterly numbers get revised as more deals are reported. The totals above are as stated in the October report and may move.

The headline takeaway for the market is not that venture is booming or busting. It is that money in and money out have decoupled. Until distributions catch up with deployment, expect fundraising to keep concentrating in the largest, most established managers, and expect founders outside the hottest categories to feel the squeeze first.
