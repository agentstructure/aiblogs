## What happened

A securities-law quirk that most issuers had never heard of reached its first real deadline this week. Under the SEC's "innovation exemption," a new type of trading venue can list tokenized versions of exchange-traded stock without the issuer's consent, unless the issuer objects in writing within 30 days of receiving notice. For companies that received notice on October 4, that window closed on Tuesday, November 3, according to [CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/business/2026/11/03/amazon-tesla-and-spacex-join-okx-ice-tokenized-stock-objections-as-deadline-looms).

The venue is OKXICE, a joint venture between the crypto exchange OKX and Intercontinental Exchange (ICE), the parent of the NYSE. CoinDesk reports that its November 2 notice added six objectors: Amazon (AMZN), Tesla (TSLA), SpaceX, Netflix (NFLX), SoFi Technologies (SOFI) and Lam Research (LRCX). That brought the disclosed total to 56, up from 50 in the prior Friday update.

[Crypto.news](https://crypto.news/amazon-tesla-join-56-firms-opposing-okxice-tokenized-stock-plans/) reports the same tally. Both outlets say OKXICE originally picked 62 companies, and 49 of them, about 79%, have objected. Seven more objectors were not on the original list. Crypto.news notes that its own text is not fully consistent on the earlier count, and reports use different baselines, so treat the exact numbers as a snapshot of one notice rather than a final tally.

## How the opt-out works

The order was issued on September 17, 2026, and runs five years, through September 17, 2031, [according to Davis Polk](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/sec-s-innovation-exemption-opens-path-tokenized-stock-trading). The Commission can modify it. The mechanics matter more for issuers than the headline count:

- **Notice goes to the cover-page address.** The venue must send written notice to the principal executive office listed on the cover of the issuer's Exchange Act reports, and must wait at least 30 calendar days before trading the token.
- **Silence is consent.** The objection must be in writing and delivered to the venue by the 30th day. Davis Polk notes late objections are not provided for, and an issuer that misses the window cannot later block trading on that venue.
- **Each venue is separate.** An objection applies only to the venue that received it, so an issuer may have to respond to several notices.
- **Issuer-sponsored tokens are different.** The opt-out covers only tokens created by third parties. Issuers have no objection right over their own tokenized shares, and an objection does not stop a company from tokenizing its own stock or joining other arrangements, [CoinDesk and crypto.news](https://crypto.news/amazon-tesla-join-56-firms-opposing-okxice-tokenized-stock-plans/) both note.
- **No central registry.** [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/10/how-public-companies-should-prepare-for-tokenized-securities) points out that no public repository tracks objections, which puts the monitoring burden on the company.

Venues have five more business days to publicly disclose objections, so the count can still move after the deadline, CoinDesk says.

## What the exemption requires of venues

The conditions are not trivial, which is part of why this is a regulatory story and not just a crypto one. Per Davis Polk, a venue must be permissioned, use automated market maker liquidity pools, run on a public blockchain with auditable smart contracts, and admit only verified users. It may not offer leverage, borrowing or credit to participants, and it may not host primary issuance or initial offerings.

There are also volume limits. Tier 1 is capped at 75 symbols and 0.25% of average daily volume per stock; Tier 2 allows 250 symbols and 2.5%. Trading halts must match those of the primary listing exchange. Third-party tokenizers must deliver proxy materials and other issuer communications to tokenholders at no cost to the issuer or the holders, and tokens must carry the same dividend, voting and liquidation rights as the underlying stock.

OKXICE itself plans round-the-clock trading of tokenized US equities, settled in stablecoins, with prices set by pools of assets rather than an order book, CoinDesk reports. Co-chairman Andrew Cuomo said the project is at "the very beginning of the rollout of fundamentally different market structure," and gave no launch date or stock count.

## Why capital raisers should care

Most readers of this site are not a mega-cap with a mailroom and a general counsel. Three takeaways still apply.

**First, the exemption reaches any company whose stock trades on a national exchange.** If you are a small-cap or recent IPO issuer, a venue can serve notice on you just as it can on Amazon. The companies that objected are mostly large, but nothing in the order limits notices to them. The 13 original picks with no disclosed objection, according to CoinDesk, include Apple (AAPL), Coinbase (COIN), Robinhood (HOOD), Palantir (PLTR) and Boeing (BA), and Securitize and BitGo told CoinDesk they did not plan to object.

**Second, the ownership question is a financing question.** Morgan Lewis tells issuers to think through investor visibility, shareholder records, dividends, voting, proxy delivery, price dislocation and cybersecurity before the clock runs out. Those are the same issues that surface in a follow-on offering, an ATM program or a tender offer: who your holders are, how you reach them, and whether trading in your stock behaves the way your disclosures assume. Tokens trading against automated pools outside the primary listing hours could create price moves you did not plan for, and Davis Polk notes that the order does not require issuers to cooperate with third-party tokenizers.

**Third, the process is the real risk.** A 30-day notice that lands in the wrong inbox is a legal decision made by default. Morgan Lewis recommends that mailroom, investor relations and corporate secretary staff escalate any venue notice to legal immediately, that the objection deadline be calendared, and that someone be assigned authority to file. Coordinate with your transfer agent and proxy provider too, because record dates and corporate actions have to work across both on-chain and off-chain holders.

## What to watch

Five items bear watching over the next few weeks.

1. **Late disclosures.** Venues can publish objections for five business days after a deadline, so the 56 figure may change.
2. **Launch details.** Cuomo gave no launch date or list of stocks. The final roster of tokenized names, and whether the objectors cut it down to a small group, will show how much issuer pushback matters.
3. **Other venues.** The order allows any qualifying venue to serve notice, and an objection to one does not bind the others. Expect more notices, and more 30-day clocks, for issuers.
4. **SEC adjustments.** Davis Polk notes the Commission can modify the exemption, and the order does not address withdrawing an objection or restarting the notice clock. Both gaps could be tested.
5. **Issuer-sponsored tokens.** Companies that object to third parties but tokenize their own shares would keep control of the product. Whether any of the 56 take that route is the next signal.

None of this is a recommendation to object or not to object; that depends on your shareholder base, your transfer agent and your counsel. The practical point is narrower. For the first time, an SEC exemption makes an issuer's silence a legal act, and November 3 was the first day that showed how many chose to speak.
