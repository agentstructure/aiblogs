## What was announced

On Nov. 9, DoubleU Games (KRX: 192080) and DoubleDown Interactive (DDI) [agreed to an all-cash tender offer](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/11/09/3398269/0/en/index.html) at US$16.25 per American depositary share. DoubleU, DoubleDown's controlling shareholder, wants every ADS it does not already own. According to [DoubleDown's filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001799567/000119312526453845/d163036dex991.htm), DoubleU holds about 67.05% of DoubleDown's common shares, and each ADS represents one-twentieth of a common share.

If the tender succeeds, DoubleU has agreed to buy the remaining shares in a second-step transaction under the Korean Commercial Act at the same per-ADS price. The offer replaces an earlier DoubleU proposal. DoubleDown's [quarterly filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001799567/000162828026072885/ddi-20260930_d2.htm) says the board, acting on a special committee's recommendation, unanimously approved the agreement and recommends that ADS holders tender.

This is a take-private by a parent that already controls the company, which is a different animal from a rival bid. The question for investors is less "who else might show up" and more "what do the minority holders think the business is worth."

## The conditions that decide it

Two conditions carry the weight, and the filing says neither can be waived outright.

- **Ownership threshold.** After the offer, DoubleU must own at least 95% of DoubleDown's common shares. DoubleU can lower that to no less than 90%, but only with DoubleDown's prior written consent, which the filing says cannot be unreasonably withheld, conditioned or delayed.
- **Majority of the minority.** More than 50% of the ADSs held by unaffiliated holders must be validly tendered. This one is non-waivable.

A third condition, that the board's recommendation stands, can be waived by DoubleU alone. The offer has no financing condition.

The 95% line matters because it is what makes the squeeze-out clean. With a holder already near 67%, the roughly 33% remainder has to be coaxed mostly into one transaction. Holders who sit out risk being cashed out later at the same price anyway, but that is the mechanism's design, not a promise about how any holder will behave. The filings I reviewed do not give a tender expiry date, so check the formal offer documents when they are filed.

## How DoubleU is paying

The offer is funded with cash on hand and [a bridge loan facility of up to KRW300 billion](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/doubleu-games-agrees-to-buy-remaining-stake-in-doubledown-interactive/), which PocketGamer.biz converts to about $224 million. The [filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001799567/000119312526453845/d163036dex991.htm) names Korea Investment & Securities as mandated lead arranger, facility agent and security agent. Kroll's Duff & Phelps Opinions Practice gave the special committee a fairness opinion.

Reported total consideration is about $265 million, according to [investgame.net](https://investgame.net/news/doubleu-games-is-taking-doubledown-interactive-private-at-a-77-premium-paying-1-6x-ltm-ebitda/). I could not confirm that total in the primary documents I could open, and the filing I read does not give an ADS count, so treat it as a trade-press figure. The same report says DoubleU would fund roughly $75 million from its own cash plus the bridge loan. Those numbers are one outlet's account, not a company statement.

## What the business looks like

DoubleDown runs social casino games and a smaller iGaming line. In the [quarter ended Sept. 30](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001799567/000162828026072885/ddi-20260930_d2.htm), the company reported:

- Total revenue of $94.3 million, down 1.6% from $95.8 million a year earlier.
- Social casino revenue of $76.8 million, down 3.5%.
- iGaming revenue of $17.4 million, up 7.4%.
- Operating profit of $37.5 million, up 7.2%.
- Net income of $18.4 million, down 44.0% from $32.8 million.

The net income drop sits well below the operating line, so it comes from items beneath operating profit rather than from the games themselves. The filing excerpt I reviewed does not break that out, so I won't guess at the cause.

The balance sheet is the striking part. DoubleDown reported cash and cash equivalents of $483.4 million at Sept. 30, up from $348.3 million a year earlier, plus $98.6 million of short-term investments. By my own addition, that is about $582 million of cash and short-term investments. Set that beside the reported deal value of roughly $265 million and the arithmetic suggests why a buyer, and perhaps a rival bidder, would care about the cash pile. Note that DoubleU already owns about two-thirds of that cash through its stake, and the combination of two sources here is my calculation, not a figure either company published.

## The bidding history, with a caveat

investgame.net reports that DoubleU first proposed $11.25 per share in April, that an unnamed US-listed strategic company later offered $15.00 for all of DoubleDown, and that DoubleU described $16.25 as its best and final offer. It also reports a later $17.00 approach from that rival that came without clear financing. None of this appears in the filings I could read, which say only that the new offer replaces a prior proposal. The rival is unnamed, so treat the sequence as unconfirmed reporting rather than established fact.

If it is accurate, it frames the special committee's job: it had to weigh a controlling holder's last offer against a possible competing bid that the controller was unlikely to support.

## Why it matters beyond one ticker

Controller buyouts of listed minorities are a recurring form of gaming consolidation, and the safeguards here are the standard ones: an independent special committee, a fairness opinion and a vote of unaffiliated holders. For investors in small-cap gaming names with a dominant parent, the DoubleDown process is a useful template for how much leverage minority holders actually have, and how a board weighs a controller's final price against uncertain alternatives.

## What to watch

- **The formal offer documents.** They should set the expiry, the exact premium disclosure and the procedure for tendering ADSs.
- **Participation.** The majority-of-the-minority test is the one hurdle DoubleU cannot waive away, and the 95% threshold can be lowered only with DoubleDown's consent.
- **Revenue direction.** Social casino revenue is shrinking modestly while iGaming grows from a much smaller base. Whether that mix improves is a driver of what the business is worth after the deal.
- **Capital use.** With a bridge loan in the structure, how DoubleU later services that debt, possibly from the cash DoubleDown holds, will be a topic once the deal closes.
- **Delisting.** A successful squeeze-out would end DDI's Nasdaq listing, removing one of the few US-listed pure-play social casino names.

This is information and commentary, not investment advice. Anyone holding DDI ADSs should read the tender documents and the special committee's reasoning directly.
