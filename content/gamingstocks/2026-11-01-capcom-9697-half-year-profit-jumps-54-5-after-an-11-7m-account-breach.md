## What happened

Capcom (TSE: 9697) reported results for the six months ended Sept. 30 on Oct. 27, and the headline numbers were strong. According to the company's [financial results filing](https://www.capcom.co.jp/ir/english/data/pdf/result/2026/2nd/result_2026_2nd_01.pdf), net sales rose 49.0% to 120.9 billion yen and operating profit rose 54.5% to 60.8 billion yen. Profit attributable to owners of the parent climbed 56.6% to 43.1 billion yen.

Five days before that, the company had disclosed a security incident. In a [notice dated Oct. 22](https://www.capcom.co.jp/ir/english/news/html/e261022.html), Capcom said an unauthorized third party accessed CAPCOM ID, its shared account system, after suspicious activity was detected on Oct. 19. The two stories sit side by side for investors: a very good half, and a customer-trust problem whose cost has not been spelled out.

## The first half in numbers

The growth came almost entirely from Digital Contents. That segment's sales rose 70.8% to 85.1 billion yen and its operating profit rose 66.7% to 52.3 billion yen, per the filing. Total game unit sales reached 36.86 million, up from 23.85 million a year earlier, across 260 titles in 239 countries and regions.

Most of that volume was not new. Catalog titles sold 32.18 million units, against 22.85 million in the same period last year. Subtracting that from the total leaves roughly 4.7 million units from new releases (my arithmetic from the filing's two figures). The new releases the company named were the all-new PRAGMATA, at more than 2.7 million units, and Onimusha: Way of the Sword, at more than 1.7 million.

The back catalog did the heavy lifting:

- Resident Evil Requiem passed 8 million cumulative units, which Capcom attributes partly to "active pricing measures."
- Devil May Cry 5 passed 14 million after its June Switch 2 release.
- Past Monster Hunter titles sold more after Capcom announced a 2027 window for the Monster Hunter Wilds: Ascendance expansion and Switch 2 versions of both games.

Operating margin was about 50.3% on my calculation (60,769 divided by 120,887), up from roughly 48.5% a year earlier. Arcade Operations grew sales 26.2% and Amusement Equipments 8.9%, while Other Businesses slipped 4.7%. Operating cash flow was 36.8 billion yen versus 4.5 billion yen a year earlier, according to [Gaming.net's read of the filing](https://www.gaming.net/capcom-half-year-net-sales-rise-49-as-operating-profit-climbs/). Capcom set an interim dividend of 23 yen per share, payable Nov. 16.

## Why the guidance did not move

Capcom left its full-year forecast unchanged from May 13: net sales of 210 billion yen, operating profit of 83 billion yen and net profit of 58 billion yen. The company's [release](https://www.capcom.co.jp/ir/english/news/html/e261027b.html) says it is "making steady progress" toward that forecast.

Set against the first half, that forecast is easy to clear. Six months in, Capcom has booked 57.6% of the sales target, 73.2% of the operating profit target and 74.3% of the net profit target. For the second half to merely meet the forecast, it would need sales of about 89.1 billion yen and operating profit of about 22.2 billion yen.

Using the growth rates in the forecast to back out last year's figures (again my arithmetic, so treat it as approximate), second-half operating profit would have to fall by around 38% year on year, and second-half sales by around 22%. Two readings are possible. Management may be holding a deliberately conservative number, or the second half may be thinner on new content than the first. The filing's own text points to catalog strength, not a stacked release schedule. Titles on the calendar include the Monster Hunter Wilds Switch 2 port, which Capcom's [news page](https://www.capcom.co.jp/ir/english/news/) lists for Dec. 4, and the mobile launch of Monster Hunter Outlanders on Oct. 29. Neither is a PRAGMATA-sized launch.

The honest takeaway is that the guidance gap is a question, not an answer. A formal raise at the next report would be the clearest sign that management saw the conservatism too.

## The breach: size, and what is not known

The scale is large. Capcom's notice counts 11,774,247 records worldwide containing email addresses and system data such as account creation time, last login time, login count and last-login IP address. A further 43 records included date of birth, gender, country or region, and nickname. Attackers changed passwords on 196 accounts, which Capcom reset. Capcom says credit card information was not affected, and that its games and CAPCOM ID services remain available. [BleepingComputer](https://bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/capcom-confirms-data-breach-affecting-117-million-accounts) reported the same figures and noted that the company has not shared how the attacker first got in.

For an investor, three things matter:

1. **Financial cost is unquantified.** Capcom's half-year filing reported no change to its forecast, and the pages I reviewed contain no breach-related charge or estimate. Any costs for notification, support channels or regulatory action would show up in later reports.
2. **The data is mostly emails and metadata.** That points to phishing risk for players rather than payment fraud, which limits direct liability but not reputational damage.
3. **The count may change.** Capcom said the figures could be revised as its investigation continues.

## What to watch next

The broader context is a publisher cycle running hot at the top. Take-Two (TTWO) has kept its fiscal 2027 net bookings outlook at $8.0 billion to $8.2 billion, which [Reuters reported](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/two-sticks-annual-bookings-outlook-114234190.html) was below the $8.86 billion analysts expected, ahead of Grand Theft Auto VI's planned November release. Capcom shows what a catalog-heavy publisher can earn when pricing, platform ports and a few strong new franchises line up, with no single mega-launch.

Three things to track:

- **Capcom's second-half cadence.** Whether Switch 2 versions of Monster Hunter Wilds and its expansion lift catalog sales as the first half suggests.
- **Breach follow-ups.** Any revised record count, regulatory action in Japan, or a disclosed cost.
- **Guidance language.** Whether Capcom keeps calling its progress "steady" or moves to raise the numbers when it reports next.

None of this changes the central fact. Capcom earned half of every yen it sold in the first half, and its own forecast implies the rest of the year slows sharply. Which of those two is the surprise will decide how the market reads the next report.
