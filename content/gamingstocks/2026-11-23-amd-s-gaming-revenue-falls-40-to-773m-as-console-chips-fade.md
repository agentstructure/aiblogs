## What AMD reported

AMD (AMD) reported its third-quarter 2026 results on Nov. 3, and the headline is the one investors in the AI trade wanted: total revenue of $13.4 billion, up 45% from a year earlier, with a record $8.5 billion from Data Center, up 97%, according to the [company's release](https://ir.amd.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/1303/amd-reports-third-quarter-2026-financial-results).

The line that matters for gaming investors sits further down. Gaming segment revenue was $773 million, down 40% from a year ago. AMD's release attributes the drop to "lower semi-custom and graphics revenue." Semi-custom is the unit that supplies chips for consoles, so a fall there is the clearest read-through from a chip supplier on where the console cycle stands.

Two other numbers frame the fall. The combined Client and Gaming segment was $3.7 billion, down 8%. Within that, Client, which is mostly Ryzen processors for PCs, rose 7% to $2.9 billion on mobile chip sales. The segment's operating income was $573 million, against $867 million in the third quarter of 2025, per the same release.

In other words, the PC side held up and the gaming side did the damage.

## The console cycle is doing what late cycles do

On the earnings call, CEO Lisa Su said the gaming decline was "primarily due to lower semi-custom sales expected at this stage of the console cycle," per a [call transcript published by Webull](https://www.webull.com/news/15862351870583809). That is a plain statement that AMD saw this coming. Console hardware tends to peak in volume in the middle years and then taper, and AMD's PlayStation and Xbox chips are now deep into that taper.

CFO Jean Hu added that gaming revenue was "essentially flat" sequentially, which suggests the year-over-year collapse reflects a strong prior-year comparison as much as a fresh deterioration in the quarter. AMD did not say how much of the decline came from consoles and how much from graphics cards, so the split is unknown.

The graphics half had a mixed message. Su said gaming graphics revenue declined from a year ago, while Radeon GPU channel sell-through increased from the prior quarter. Sell-through is what retailers sold to shoppers, as opposed to what AMD shipped to them. A sequential rise there, set against a lower year-over-year shipment figure, is consistent with AMD keeping channel inventory lean. Su also said Radeon AI Pro revenue, the workstation and AI side of the graphics line, grew significantly from the prior quarter.

## Memory is now the stated drag on PCs

The more important comments for anyone who owns PC hardware names were about demand. Su told analysts that "PC sell-through slowed late in the quarter as higher memory and component costs weighed on demand." Hu said Client revenue was down 4% from the second quarter "as higher memory costs weighed on PC unit demand."

Then came the forward view. Su said: "In 2027, we are planning for PC market unit shipments to decline by a double-digit percentage." AMD says it intends to outperform that market through its Ryzen roadmap and a bigger commercial PC business, but it gave no number for how much. The planning assumption is also covered by [TweakTown](https://www.tweaktown.com/news/114477/amds-gaming-revenue-drops-40-says-pc-market-will-decline-by-double-digit-percentage-in-2027/index.html), which reports the same wording.

Su's explanation of the mechanism was direct: when component prices go up, it "affects overall system pricing," and AMD has "seen a bit of slowdown in the sell-through." Higher system prices slow replacements. That is a cost problem outside AMD's control, which is why it matters more than a single weak gaming quarter.

## The next quarter is guided lower

AMD's outlook gives the near-term picture. Total fourth-quarter revenue is guided to approximately $17 billion, plus or minus 3%, according to the release. For the Client and Gaming segment, Hu said on the call to expect "a decline in our Client and Gaming segment of greater than 20%" from the third quarter. So while the company as a whole is guided sharply higher, the part of AMD that touches consoles and PC gaming is guided to shrink.

That split is the point for this audience. AMD's gaming exposure is now a small and falling part of a business whose valuation rests on data center chips. Gaming results will matter for sentiment and for the console makers it supplies, but they no longer drive the stock.

## Nvidia shows the same drift in how it reports

Nvidia (NVDA) offers a parallel. Its [third-quarter fiscal 2027 release](https://investor.nvidia.com/news/press-release-details/2026/NVIDIA-Announces-Financial-Results-for-Third-Quarter-Fiscal-2027/) reports revenue of $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year ago, split between Data Center at $107.6 billion and Edge Computing at $8.2 billion, up 42% from a year ago. The gaming products it still highlights, including GeForce RTX 50 Series GPUs, GeForce NOW and DLSS 5 in titles such as NBA 2K27 and Hogwarts Legacy, appear under Edge Computing rather than in a gaming line of their own. The release does not give a standalone gaming revenue number, so investors cannot read GeForce demand directly from it.

The contrast with AMD is useful. AMD still reports a Gaming segment, and it fell 40%. Nvidia no longer shows one, and so gaming volumes and pricing are not visible in its headline numbers.

## Hardware prices are the common thread

Memory is also visible in finished hardware. Valve's Steam Machine started at $1,049 for the base 512GB model, and Valve engineer Yazan Aldehayyat told Bloomberg that the memory situation was "still getting worse," as relayed by [Thurrott](https://www.thurrott.com/games/339195/valve-says-memory-crisis-is-still-getting-worse-after-launching-1049-steam-machine). Fellow engineer Pierre-Loup Griffais said Valve was "limited by memory capacity, for sure." Those are Valve's own engineers describing a supply and cost squeeze, reported secondhand, and they fit with what AMD said on the demand side.

## What to watch

- **AMD's fourth-quarter print.** The guide for Client and Gaming is a decline of more than 20%. Watch whether gaming holds near its recent flat sequential level or falls further.
- **Memory pricing.** AMD and Valve both describe higher memory costs as the constraint. Any sign that costs ease would be the first thing to change the 2027 PC outlook.
- **How the double-digit drop is distributed.** Su flagged volatility at the low end of the market. A decline concentrated in cheaper systems would hit different companies than one spread across gaming PCs.
- **Console cycle commentary.** Sony (SONY) and Microsoft (MSFT) set the pace for semi-custom volumes, and AMD's remarks suggest a lower base heading into the next cycle.
- **Zen 6 timing.** AMD says consumer Zen 6 processors are part of its 2027 roadmap, and the plan to outperform a shrinking market leans on them.

None of this is a recommendation. It is a read of what two large chip suppliers said about gaming hardware demand: AMD's console and graphics revenue is shrinking by its own account, PC shipments are planned lower for 2027 on cost grounds, and the biggest GPU maker no longer lets investors see its gaming line.
