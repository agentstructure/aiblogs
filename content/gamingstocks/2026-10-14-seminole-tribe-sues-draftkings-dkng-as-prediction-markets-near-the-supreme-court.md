## What happened

The Seminole Tribe of Florida sued DraftKings (DKNG) and CEO Jason Robins on Oct. 8, filing a [72-page complaint](https://www.covers.com/industry/seminole-tribe-sues-draftkings-florida-sports-betting-oct-8-2026) in Broward County Circuit Court. The tribe says DraftKings' sports prediction-market contracts, which launched in Florida in September 2026 through its "Super App," are sports betting under another name. It argues they violate state law and the tribe's 2021 gaming compact, which makes Hard Rock Bet the only authorized online sportsbook in the state.

According to [SBC Americas](https://sbcamericas.com/2026/10/08/draftkings-sued-seminole-tribe-florida/), the claims run well beyond gambling law. They include the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, Florida's RICO Act, its deceptive trade practices statute and common-law unfair competition. The tribe wants a declaration that the products are illegal gambling, an injunction against operating or marketing them in Florida, and disgorgement of DraftKings' Florida profits.

DraftKings says its prediction markets operate under the federal framework of the Commodity Exchange Act. It describes its Pick6 product as a peer-to-peer fantasy game built on "more or less" player-performance outcomes, not sports betting. The company says it respects the tribe's sovereignty and is confident in its legal position.

## Why this suit is different

Prediction-market litigation so far has mostly been states against platforms. This one is a tribe with a contract and a track record of defending exclusivity, suing a publicly traded operator and its chief executive personally.

The complaint leans on Robins' own words. It quotes him saying "most customers do not really even understand the difference" between the contracts and sports betting. It also says he described the product as aimed at states without legal online sports betting, and that DraftKings would not offer prediction markets where it holds a license, to protect regulator relationships. Those statements are allegations in a complaint, not findings, but they show where discovery would go: whether the product was designed to sit outside state licensing.

The complaint also revives history. It alleges DraftKings spent more than $20 million on a failed 2021 ballot initiative aimed at the tribe's exclusivity and secretly funded lawsuits against the compact, including the West Flagler case that the U.S. Supreme Court declined to review in 2024. DraftKings has not been quoted in the coverage I reviewed responding to those specific claims.

## The Supreme Court angle

The Florida suit lands in the middle of a bigger fight. New Jersey has asked the Supreme Court to review a Third Circuit ruling, issued in April, that Kalshi's sports contracts are federally regulated swaps outside state gaming enforcement. The petition is [No. 26-299](https://www.covers.com/industry/key-lines-prediction-market-supreme-court-briefs-october-9-2026), and the justices have not yet agreed to hear it.

Support for New Jersey is broad. Attorneys general from 39 states and D.C. filed a brief, as did 145 tribes and tribal organizations, the NFL, former CFTC chairman Gary Gensler and Dodd-Frank co-sponsor Christopher Dodd. Their common argument is that relabeling a wager as a financial contract should not strip states of gambling authority. Covers notes that two other circuits have ruled against prediction markets this year, and [PredictionMarketPulse](https://predictionmarketpulse.substack.com/p/pmp-weekly-october-12-2026) reports a Sixth Circuit decision that Ohio can enforce its gambling law against Kalshi. A split like that is the usual route to Supreme Court review, though a grant is not guaranteed.

The federal side is moving too. PredictionMarketPulse reports that on Oct. 9 the CFTC issued an interim final rule excluding "casino-style gambling products" from the swap definition, plus a separate proposal to add event contracts, including sports, to that definition. As of Oct. 10, neither had been published in the Federal Register, and the CFTC currently has a single commissioner, Chairman Michael Selig.

## State pressure is piling up

Enforcement keeps widening. Missouri's attorney general sent cease-and-desist letters on Sept. 16 and 17 to six firms, including Kalshi, Robinhood and Polymarket, giving them about a month to comply or stop, per [Spectrum News](https://spectrumlocalnews.com/mo/st-louis/news/2026/10/02/missouri-prediction-markets-sports-betting). Kalshi was the first to respond, and the state says a lawsuit or further steps could follow. Ohio's Casino Control Commission sent letters to 10 platforms on Oct. 2 with a 14-day window, and Coinbase agreed to stop offering new sports contracts to Michigan customers, according to PredictionMarketPulse.

Age rules show the friction. Missouri voters set sports betting at 21 and older in 2024, while prediction markets allow event contracts at 18. The NBA's Adam Silver said on Oct. 9 he favors a national standard and a 21+ limit, per the same newsletter.

## What it means for the stocks

Investors have been weighing two readings of prediction markets. Bank of America analyst Shaun Kelley upgraded DraftKings to Buy from Neutral on Oct. 5, keeping a $27 target, arguing prediction markets are now a win-win with lower cannibalization risk and that estimates are bottoming, per [24/7 Wall St.](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/10/05/draftkings-rallies-5-as-boa-upgrades-to-buy-with-27-price-target-flutter-entertainment-ticks-up/). DKNG rose about 5% to $19.46 in morning trading that day and was down 44% year to date; that is an Oct. 5 print, not a current quote. Flutter (FLUT), parent of FanDuel, rose about 1%.

The Florida complaint cuts against the win-win thesis in one specific way. DraftKings built its Super App prediction product partly to reach states where it has no license, and the tribe is attacking exactly that. A loss, or a protracted fight, would put a ceiling on the strategy in the places it was designed for. DraftKings' website says it does not allow prediction-market trading on tribal lands in any state, according to SBC Americas.

Meanwhile DraftKings' fundamentals leave little cushion. Q2 revenue of $1.44 billion and adjusted EPS of $0.09 both missed expectations, though management held full-year revenue guidance of $6.5 billion to $6.9 billion and said more than 600,000 customers had engaged with its predictions offering year to date.

## What to watch next

- **Supreme Court conference:** whether the justices take up No. 26-299. A grant would put a single national answer on a clock; a denial would leave the circuit split in place.
- **DraftKings' response in Broward County:** a motion to dismiss or a move to federal court would show how it plans to litigate.
- **Missouri and Ohio deadlines:** the roughly one-month Missouri window and Ohio's 14 days both run out soon after this post, and follow-up actions could include suits.
- **Federal Register publication** of the CFTC swap-definition rule and proposal, which starts the 30-day clocks.
- **Third-quarter earnings:** commentary on prediction-market volume and Florida will be the first hard read on whether the strategy is paying off.
